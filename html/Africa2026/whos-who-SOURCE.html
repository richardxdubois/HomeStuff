How to Use This

And why there are no photographs in it

This follows the six green watch-list boxes in the field companion, one section each, mammals before birds. It is deliberately not a picture book — you already own one of those. Your Merlin packs hold photographs for every bird in here, and the job of this document is to tell you which of the eleven hundred species in those packs is worth looking up, and what to look at when you do.

Because in practice nobody identifies an animal from a photograph. You identify it from two or three marks, a posture and a behaviour, which is what each entry gives you. A picture of a serval tells you less than a cat on absurdly long legs with huge upright ears, hunting by ear in the crater grass, pouncing vertically.

★ marks the prizes — the ones worth asking a guide about by name. ⚑ marks endemics and near-endemics, which are prizes of another kind: this is the only place you can see them.

Every name links to an iNaturalist search, which covers mammals as well as birds and will give you photographs on the iPad. They are search links rather than deep links, so an ambiguous name returns a short list rather than a dead end.

A seventh section, The Ones You’ll Stop Noticing, covers the birds and animals you will see a hundred times a day and never learn the names of. They are absent from the watch-lists because those were built around prizes and endemics — which is precisely the wrong filter for a first safari, where the commonest things are what you will actually ask about.

The boxes are for ticking, and the list feeds straight into eBird afterwards — where checklists from the Boteti and Linyanti are a genuine, if small, contribution to a thin record.

Northern Tanzania

15–18 August · Manyara, Ngorongoro, Tarangire

MAMMALS

  • ☐Black rhino ★ — Ngorongoro. Two horns, the front longer. The clincher against white rhino is the lip: hooked and prehensile for browsing, not square for grazing. Head carried high, almost always alone. At distance, a grey boulder that moves — watch for the ears swivelling independently.
  • ☐Tree-climbing lion ★ — Manyara. Ordinary lions with an unexplained habit. Don't scan the ground — look along the horizontal limbs of acacias and sausage trees three to five metres up, for dangling legs and a draped tail.
  • ☐Serval — Crater grassland. A cat the size of a small dog on absurdly long legs, with huge upright ears and bold black spots on tawny. Hunts by ear, then pounces vertically — sometimes three metres.
  • ☐Golden and black-backed jackal — Both on the crater floor. Black-backed is rich rufous with a sharply defined dark saddle; golden (side-striped) is duller fawn with a bushier, white-tipped tail.
  • ☐Spotted hyena — Sloping back because the forelegs are longer, blunt muzzle, irregular blotches. The rising whoop at night is unmistakable.
  • ☐Fringe-eared oryx — Tarangire. Grey-fawn with a black facial mask and flank stripe, long straight ringed horns, and small dark tufts on the ear tips that give it the name.
  • ☐Lesser kudu — Tarangire, thick bush. Smaller and greyer than greater kudu, with eleven to fifteen crisp white body stripes and two white throat patches. Very shy.
  • ☐Gerenuk — Tarangire. Unmistakable by posture alone — an impossibly long neck and legs, tiny wedge of a head, and it feeds standing upright on its hind legs against a bush.
  • ☐Dwarf mongoose — Africa's smallest carnivore, barely 30 cm including tail. Colonies live in disused termite mounds; look for one sentinel upright while the rest forage.
  • ☐African elephant — Tarangire holds some of Tanzania's largest dry-season herds along the river.

BIRDS

  • ☐Secretarybird ★ — An eagle on crane's legs, 1.3 m tall, grey with black thighs and a spray of quill-like head plumes. Walks twenty or thirty kilometres a day and kills snakes by stamping. If you doubt it's a raptor, look at the hooked bill.
  • ☐Kori bustard ★ — The heaviest flying bird alive; big males approach 19 kg. Finely barred grey neck, black crest, sandy back, and a gait of enormous deliberation. Displaying males inflate the throat into a white balloon.
  • ☐Grey crowned crane — Golden bristle crown, slate-grey body, white cheek panel, red gular sac. Often in pairs on the crater floor.
  • ☐Greater and lesser flamingo — Manyara, usually distant. Greater is paler with a pink bill tipped black; lesser is deeper pink with a bill so dark red it reads as black.
  • ☐Lilac-breasted roller — Lilac throat and breast, turquoise belly, and in flight a flash of electric blue. Perches conspicuously on bare snags — the bird everyone photographs on day one.
  • ☐Superb starling — Iridescent blue-green above, orange below, and the clincher is a narrow white breast band dividing the two. Absolutely everywhere; don't stop noticing it.
  • ☐Ostrich — Males black and white, females uniformly grey-brown.
  • ☐Hamerkop — Brown, with a hammer-shaped head from the backward crest. Walks hunched beside water. Builds a domed stick fortress over a metre across — and your Arusha hotel is named for it.
  • ☐Rufous-tailed weaver ⚑ — Tanzania endemic. Scaly grey-brown with a pale eye and rufous tail, foraging on the ground in noisy parties. Tarangire is the classic place.
  • ☐Ashy starling ⚑ — Near-endemic. Long-tailed and uniformly dusty grey-brown — conspicuously the one starling with no iridescence at all.
  • ☐Yellow-collared lovebird ⚑ — Near-endemic. Small green parrot with a black head and a yellow collar, usually detected by the noise as a flock rockets past.

The Ngorongoro Rim Forest

15–16 August · the one montane habitat on the trip

BIRDS

  • ☐Schalow’s turaco ★ — The prize of the rim, and worth the walk on its own. Coppery jade-green shading to a dark iridescent blue toward the tail, with a white-tipped crest, feeding on fruit high in the canopy. Heard far more often than seen — a harsh barking series — so listen upward and look for movement rather than colour.
  • ☐Hartlaub’s turaco ⚑ — Near-endemic to Tanzania. Similar shape, deep glossy blue-green with a white line before the eye and a crimson flash in the wing when it flies. Turacos are poor fliers and prefer to run along branches, which is often how you spot one.
  • ☐Mbulu white-eye ⚑ — Local and scarce. Small and sharp-billed, olive-green above, yellow below, with the broad white eye-ring that names it. Works the canopy in restless little parties.
  • ☐Bar-tailed trogon ★ — Sits absolutely motionless and vertical on a shaded branch, which is why it is missed — metallic green head, crimson belly, and a finely barred black-and-white tail seen from below. Look for the shape, not the movement.
  • ☐The montane sunbirds ★ — A whole guild found nowhere else on your route. Tacazze, violet-bronze and long-tailed. Golden-winged, with yellow wing and tail edges. Malachite, brilliant green with long central tail feathers in breeding dress. Green-headed and eastern double-collared, the latter with a scarlet breast band above a yellow belly. All feeding at forest flowers, all iridescent, all worth stopping for.
  • ☐Cinnamon-chested bee-eater — Green with a rich cinnamon breast and a black mask — a forest bee-eater rather than a riverbank one, hawking from a mid-canopy perch.
  • ☐Brown-headed apalis, grey-capped warbler, Hunter’s cisticola — The small brown business of a montane forest. The cisticola is the loud one; the grey-capped warbler has a rufous face and a startlingly explosive song for its size.
  • ☐White-eyed slaty flycatcher — Slate-grey, upright, with a bold white eye-ring; sallies from an open perch and returns to it. Common and confiding around the lodge itself.
  • ☐Purple-throated cuckooshrike and brown parisoma — Both quiet skulkers in dense foliage — ask your guide, because these are found by ear.
  • ☐Augur buzzard — Over the rim rather than in the forest. Black above, white below, with a rufous tail; hangs on the updraught along the crater wall.

MAMMALS AND TREES

  • ☐Blue monkey and mantled colobus ★ — Both montane forest specialists absent from everywhere else you go. The colobus is unmistakable — black with a long white mantle and a plumed white tail, moving through the canopy in leaps. That tail is why the animal has a history. Colobus skins and tails were traded widely across East Africa as ceremonial regalia — headdresses, arm and leg ornaments, dance costume — and the demand was heavy enough to collapse populations in parts of Kenya during the colonial period. Beryl Markham mentions the tails being worn in ceremonies in West with the Night, which you may well be reading on the flight out; the monkeys on this rim survive partly because what she described as ordinary is no longer allowed.
  • ☐Buffalo and elephant — Both move through this forest, which is why an armed ranger walks with you. A lone old buffalo bull resting near the path is a routine encounter and the reason you keep formation.
  • ☐Bushbuck, duiker, bush pig — Forest antelope and pig, all shy, all more likely as a crash of undergrowth than a sighting.
  • ☐Podo, red thorn, pillarwood ★ — Learn three trees and the forest resolves. Podo is the East African yellowwood, a tall conifer with strappy dark leaves. The flat-topped red thorn is Oltepessi in Maasai. Pillarwood, also called onionwood, is the one a martial eagle will perch on. Your Maasai guide’s account of their medicinal uses is the part of this walk you cannot get anywhere else.

The Masai Mara

20–22 August · Kenya

MAMMALS

  • ☐Cheetah ★ — Small head, deep chest, and the diagnostic black tear-line running from eye to mouth. Spots are solid and round, not rosettes. Often up on a termite mound — or a vehicle bonnet.
  • ☐Leopard — Rosettes rather than spots, much heavier build, thick tail. Search the fork where a big horizontal branch meets the trunk.
  • ☐Topi — Dark mahogany with a distinct purple-blue sheen on the flanks and blackish patches on the thighs. The habit is the giveaway: standing sentinel on a termite mound.
  • ☐Coke's hartebeest — High shoulders sloping steeply away to the rump, a long narrow face, and horns forming a squat bracket shape.
  • ☐Maasai giraffe — Jagged, vine-leaf blotches running all the way down to the hooves — quite unlike the clean-edged reticulated giraffe further north.
  • ☐Blue wildebeest — The migration itself. Shaggy mane and beard, sloping back, boxy head carried low.
  • ☐Banded mongoose — Grey-brown with dark transverse bars across the back, in troops of ten to thirty.
  • ☐Bat-eared fox — Enormous ears, black mask, black legs. Hunts by listening for insects moving underground.
  • ☐Nile crocodile — The migration's principal beneficiaries; the big ones at the crossings run four to five metres.

BIRDS

  • ☐Southern ground hornbill ★ — Turkey-sized, sooty black, with startling bare red skin on the face and throat. Walks in family parties and booms at dawn like a distant drum. Females show violet-blue on the throat.
  • ☐Bateleur ★ — The tail-less eagle. Black with a chestnut back, scarlet face and legs; in flight the wings are long and the tail so abbreviated the bird looks docked. Rocks side to side as it glides, hence the French name for a tightrope walker.
  • ☐Martial eagle — Africa's heaviest eagle. Dark head and chest above a white belly finely spotted with black.
  • ☐African fish eagle — White head, breast and tail, chestnut belly, black wings. The ringing head-thrown-back cry is the sound of African water.
  • ☐Saddle-billed stork — A metre and a half tall; red bill crossed by a black band, yellow saddle at the base.
  • ☐Vultures — the guild — Lappet-faced: largest, bare pink head, the one that opens a carcass. White-backed: commonest, white lower back in flight. Rüppell's: dark with pale feather edges giving a scaled look, and holder of the altitude record for any bird.
  • ☐Yellow-billed and red-billed oxpecker — Starling-sized, brown, with stiff tails, clinging to buffalo and giraffe. Yellow-billed shows a red tip on a yellow base.

Victoria Falls

24–25 August · Zimbabwe and Zambia

MAMMALS

  • ☐Warthog — Mowing the hotel lawns with great self-possession. Grey, sparsely bristled, facial warts, and the tail held bolt upright when trotting.
  • ☐Vervet monkey — Grey body, black face with a white brow band. Bold around the terraces — guard your breakfast.
  • ☐Chacma baboon — Heavy dog-like muzzle, tail carried with a sharp kink.
  • ☐Bushbuck — Slipping through the hotel gardens at dusk. Chestnut, with white spots and stripes on the flanks and a distinctly hunched stance.

BIRDS

  • ☐Taita falcon ★ — Tiny — kestrel-sized — with a rufous nape patch and a heavy dark moustache, and very fast. Breeds on the gorge cliffs here, which makes this one of the most reliable places on earth for one of Africa's rarest raptors.
  • ☐Rock pratincole ★ — A dry-season speciality you would miss at any other time of year. Small, dark and short-billed, with a thin white collar, perching on midstream rocks in the rapids and hawking insects over the water at dusk.
  • ☐Verreaux's (black) eagle — All black with a white V on the back and a yellow face. Patrols the cliffs hunting hyrax.
  • ☐Trumpeter hornbill — Large, black and white, with a big casque — and a call disconcertingly like a crying child.
  • ☐African skimmer ★ — Tern-like, with an orange bill tipped yellow — flavirostris, “yellow-beak” — whose lower mandible is longer than the upper and flattened sideways to a blade. It flies with that lower edge ploughing the surface and snaps shut on contact, which it can do in the dark: skimmers are the only birds with vertical slit pupils, and they feed mostly at dawn and dusk. The sunset cruise is therefore the right hour. They are also the clearest wildlife casualty of the dam upstream. Skimmers nest in scrapes on exposed sandbars, and Kariba ended the Zambezi’s flood-and-silt cycle in 1958. On the middle Zambezi the population fell from 250 birds in 1987 to 36 by 1991, and a 2017 study found breeding in Zimbabwe still had not recovered more than fifty years on. Near Threatened; 15,000–25,000 left worldwide.

The Okavango Delta

26–27 August · Botswana

MAMMALS

  • ☐African wild dog ★ — Mottled black, tan and white, and no two individuals marked alike — which is how researchers tell them apart. Huge rounded ears, white tail tip. Packs of six to twenty, hunting at dawn and dusk.
  • ☐Sitatunga ★ — The swamp antelope. Shaggy oily coat, and elongated splayed hooves for walking on floating vegetation. Extremely shy: it will submerge with only its nostrils showing rather than be seen.
  • ☐Red lechwe — Bright rufous with a white belly and horns sweeping forward at the tips. Crosses water in a distinctive rocking half-swim.
  • ☐Tsessebe — Like a duller topi, and the fastest antelope in Africa.
  • ☐Southern reedbuck — Fawn, with short forward-hooked horns; gives a shrill whistle when alarmed.
  • ☐Spotted-necked otter — Small, with a mottled pale throat. Channels and lagoons.
  • ☐African elephant — Here they wade and genuinely swim between islands, trunks raised like snorkels.

BIRDS

  • ☐Pel's fishing owl ★ — The delta's great prize. An enormous ginger-rufous owl with dark barring, no ear tufts and bare legs, hunting fish at night from branches overhanging the water. Heard far more often than seen — a deep booming hoot. Ask your guide by name; the good ones know the roost trees.
  • ☐Wattled crane ★ — The tallest African crane. White neck, bare red facial skin, and the dangling white wattles that name it.
  • ☐Slaty egret ⚑ — Near-endemic to this delta. Small slate-grey heron with a rufous throat and yellow-green legs. Separate it from black egret, which fishes under a canopy made of its own wings.
  • ☐Malachite kingfisher — A thumb-sized shard of electric blue with rufous underparts and a red bill, on reed stems at eye level from the mokoro.
  • ☐African pygmy goose — Tiny, chestnut and green with a bright yellow bill, on open lily-covered water.
  • ☐African jacana — The lily-trotter: chestnut body, blue frontal shield, and absurd toes. The females are polyandrous — males incubate the eggs and carry chicks away under their wings, legs dangling.
  • ☐Saddle-billed stork and squacco heron — Both common on the floodplain edges.

Makgadikgadi and the Boteti

28–30 August · Botswana

MAMMALS

  • ☐Brown hyena ★ — Shaggy dark coat with a paler mane and legs, pointed ears. Solitary, nocturnal and far shyer than the spotted hyena — a genuinely good sighting.
  • ☐Aardwolf ★ — A small, delicate, striped relative of the hyenas weighing about ten kilos, which eats virtually nothing but termites — perhaps 250,000 in a night, lapped up with a sticky tongue. Nocturnal.
  • ☐Meerkat — Upright sentinels with dark eye patches and a thin tapering tail.
  • ☐Bat-eared fox — As in the Mara, and easier to see here on open ground.
  • ☐Gemsbok (oryx) — A bold black-and-white facial mask and rapier-straight horns; the definitive desert antelope.
  • ☐Springbok — Rufous with a bold dark side-stripe and a white face, and given to pronking — stiff-legged vertical bounds.
  • ☐Caracal — Uniform tawny, short tail, and long black ear tufts.
  • ☐Honey badger — Black with a broad grey-white saddle, and no discernible fear of anything.
  • ☐Aardvark and porcupine — Both realistic on a night drive here. Aardvark: arched back, tubular snout, rabbit ears, formidable digging claws.
  • ☐Plains zebra and blue wildebeest — The dry-season concentrations on the Boteti — the reason the hide is worth your time.

BIRDS

  • ☐Kori bustard and secretarybird — Both again, and more easily seen on this open ground than anywhere in Tanzania.
  • ☐Northern black korhaan — Males black below with white wing patches and a yellow bill, announcing themselves with a raucous clatter and refusing to be ignored.
  • ☐Double-banded courser — Cryptic sandy plover-like bird on bare ground, with two thin black breast bands. Easy to walk past.
  • ☐Fiery-necked nightjar — After dark. The whistled phrase is traditionally rendered “Good Lord, deliver us.”
  • ☐Wattled crane, saddle-billed stork, African fish eagle — All at the river; vultures ride the thermals over the plain.

Linyanti

31 August – 2 September · Botswana

MAMMALS

  • ☐Sable ★ — One of the finest animals in Africa. Mature bulls are jet black with a white face and belly, carrying massive scimitar horns swept back over the shoulders.
  • ☐Roan ★ — Larger than sable but plainer — greyish-fawn, white face patches, tufted ears, shorter backswept horns, and a curiously horse-like build.
  • ☐African wild dog ★ — Linyanti is a stronghold, and the concession allows off-road following.
  • ☐Buffalo — Big herds on the floodplain; old bulls (dagga boys) alone and mud-caked.
  • ☐Leopard — Better odds here than anywhere, helped by night drives.
  • ☐Genet — Night drives. Slender and spotted with a very long ringed tail — cat-like, but not a cat.
  • ☐African civet — Night drives. Stocky, grey with black blotches and a black facial mask, walking with the head held low.
  • ☐Spring hare — Bounds away like a tiny kangaroo; the eyeshine is a startling orange.
  • ☐Lesser bushbaby — Enormous eyes throwing the spotlight back like coins, and a wailing call that gave them the name.
  • ☐Serval, honey badger, porcupine — All possible after dark in the concession.

BIRDS

  • ☐Southern carmine bee-eater ★ — Carmine body, blue-green head, long tail streamers — and they return to these rivers to breed around August, tunnelling nest holes into sandy banks in colonies that can number hundreds. A swirling crimson blizzard over the water. Your dates fall right at the start of it.
  • ☐Pel's fishing owl ★ — A second chance, and the Linyanti is good for them.
  • ☐African skimmer — Working the channels at dusk — see the Victoria Falls entry for why this bird and the Kariba Dam are the same story.
  • ☐Meyer's parrot — Brown with yellow shoulders and a green-blue rump; usually heard first.
  • ☐Southern ground hornbill — Family parties on the floodplain.

The Ones You'll Stop Noticing

Everywhere, every day · the furniture of the bush

BIRDS

  • ☐Marabou stork ★ — Often credited with the largest wingspan of any land bird, though honestly: typical span is 2.25–2.9 m, records to 3.2 m — about a foot less than an average Andean condor. The bald head is a carrion adaptation exactly as in vultures, since feathers thrust inside a carcass would mat with blood. The white legs aren't white: that's urohidrosis, defecating on its own legs so the moisture evaporates and cools it. The pink pouch is not for food but for display and thermoregulation, connected to the left nostril and up to 45 cm long uninflated — the species name crumenifer means “carrying a purse around the neck.” And it has no voice box: the clattering is bill-rattling and the sac resonating. “Marabou” probably comes from Arabic murābit, meaning quiet. A bird named for its silence.
  • ☐Yellow-billed kite ★ — Recently split from the black kite as a species of its own. The key to both its identity and its thieving is the forked tail, used as a rudder and constantly twisting — which is how it intercepts food held by a moving human. At Ngoitokitok they don't beg, they dive-bomb. Also attends grass fires to take whatever flees them.
  • ☐Fork-tailed drongo ★ — Glossy black, deeply forked tail, red eye, and an appalling character. It mimics the alarm calls of meerkats and babblers to make them drop food it can then steal — one of the best-documented cases of tactical vocal deception in any animal. It also gives genuine alarms often enough that its victims can't simply ignore it.
  • ☐Cape turtle dove — The actual soundtrack of Africa, and nobody tells you what it is. An endlessly repeated three-note phrase traditionally rendered “work har-der, work har-der” — or, by the less industrious, “drink la-ger.” Once you've placed it you'll hear it for the rest of your life.
  • ☐Hadada ibis — Heard long before seen: an explosive, indignant ha-da-da that functions as an alarm clock across half a continent. On the ground it's a dowdy brown ibis with an iridescent green wing patch and a long decurved bill.
  • ☐Blacksmith lapwing — Bold black, white and grey, and named for the call — a sharp metallic tink, tink exactly like a hammer on an anvil. Noisy and utterly fearless near its nest.
  • ☐Grey go-away-bird — Botswana. Long-tailed, ash-grey, with a punk crest, and it does what the name says: a nasal drawn-out g'way! that announces you to everything downwind. Guides regard it with the affection reserved for a car alarm.
  • ☐Helmeted guineafowl — Absurd and everywhere. Spotted grey body, blue and red bare head with a bony casque; strongly prefers running to flying, in comic single file.
  • ☐Egyptian goose — Not a goose but a shelduck. Buff with a chocolate eye patch and a startling white wing flash in flight. Loud, quarrelsome, and on every body of water you will pass.
  • ☐Cattle egret — Small white heron that follows game and livestock to snap up what they flush. Breeding birds show buff on the crown and back.
  • ☐Red-billed and southern yellow-billed hornbill — The comic-relief hornbills, hopping about camps and picnic sites. Bill colour separates them; both are otherwise pied and long-tailed.
  • ☐Pied crow, common bulbul, African pied wagtail — The three you'll see at every camp and never look up. The bulbul's cheerful phrase is often written “quick, doctor, quick.”

MAMMALS

  • ☐Rock hyrax ★ — The guinea-pig-looking creature sunning itself on the crater rim rocks. Its closest living relatives are elephants — a fact that sounds like a party trick but shows in the anatomy, from the tiny tusk-like incisors to the structure of the feet. Also has a remarkably poor thermoregulatory system, which is why it spends so much of the morning simply lying in the sun.
  • ☐Impala — Ignored precisely because it is everywhere, which is a shame — it's a beautiful animal, and the black markings on the rump are universally known as the McDonald's logo. Rams hold harems in the dry season and make a rasping roar that first-timers reliably mistake for a predator.
  • ☐Warthog — Grey, sparsely bristled, facial warts, and the tail held bolt upright when trotting — supposedly so the family can follow through long grass. Reverses into its burrow so it can come out tusks first.
  • ☐Vervet monkey and baboon — Both bold around camps and lodges. Vervet: grey, black face, white brow band. Baboon: heavy dog-like muzzle, kinked tail. Neither should ever be fed, and both will help themselves given a doorway.
  • ☐Banded mongoose — Grey-brown with dark bars across the back, in busy troops that pour across the track in front of the vehicle.
  • ☐Tree squirrel and scrub hare — The small change of the bush. The squirrel flicks its tail and scolds; the hare bursts from under the wheels at dusk.

Our Safari Experience

Deborah and Richard Dubois

Home Animals & Birds Focus Animals Scenery & Aerials Safari Experience Victoria Falls

This page is about what it actually felt like for our safari, day to day — the bush camps, the guides, the Land Cruisers and the dust, up before six and out by 06:30, the midday siesta, tea, back out in the late afternoon. Confined to the tent during dark hours - needed escorts to meals. Ninety degrees in Botswana; much cooler everywhere else. Sleeping in became a distant memory. We had to adjust to the wildlife's active periods.

It was a long trip and there were a lot of experiences. This story is not short!

There's a progression worth capturing: discovery at the start, overwhelmed a little by how much wildlife there was and how many kinds; by Botswana, the same animals were familiar, so the interest shifted to the odd, watery situations they turned up in. The balloon over the Mara. The slick, informal bush-plane transfers — Cessnas, AirVans (flying minivans). And a quiet worry about over-tourism in the busiest spots.

To start: they say getting there is half the fun. Not this time! Suffice it to say that a missed connection in Frankfurt and lost luggage at Kilimanjaro cost us the first two days of the trip - and a lot of stress. Plus the next two nights were the coldest of the trip, at the Ngorongoro crater rim - with no warm clothes. With some artful juggling and prioritizing, in the end we didn't miss out on any significant elements of the Tanzania segment.

But enough of that - the first day in the crater erased all the angst getting there.

How did our safari work? It was a private tour - us and our driver James Msuya - for the 5 days in Tanzania. We stayed in bush camps, but did not use their guides or vehicles. Which are all Toyota Land Cruisers. These are customized by 3rd party companies locally - ours had 2 rows of seats and a pop-top. Open, it allowed us to stand, but provided shade. The side windows were open, with roll-down plastic covers in case of rain, etc.

We stayed in the Lemala Tent Camp on the rim of Ngorongoro crater, around 7600' in elevation. The Camp is all tents, with a main tent providing a lounge and dining area, and then 6 individual tents for guests. These are tents with king size beds, toilets, showers and Bush Baby hot water bottles. We had our own table in the dining tent and someone assigned to us for the duration; meals were served at the table, were excellent (content as well as presentation) and (too) abundant. Maasai locals acted as our escorts to/from our tent in the dark. Service was personalized, and they made sure to know our names and remember shared events at the camp.

The camp is in alpine forest, so in fact there was no view of the crater - just of lovely forest. The main advantage of being at the rim is first access to the crater after the Park opens. It's a 3-hr drive from outside the park, but 15' from the rim. We had the crater largely to ourselves for the morning, as there is only a handful of camps on the rim.

The tracks in Tanzania (and the Maasai Mara) are hardpacked washboard. You have to drive relatively fast on such tracks to avoid the worst of the bouncing - hence the term "African massage" (though the padding in the seats was not bad!). I found it most comfortable to stand in the Cruiser rather than sit. It gave a better view for scanning for animals,and it was easier to just roll with vehicle. A bit like riding a camel. I like to think I got more exercise that way than you'd expect. And dust, dust, dust everywhere!

It became clear that the animals were accustomed to the cars and paid little/no attention to them. So our first morning was indeed one of discovery as we ... saw our first zebra! ... saw our first lion! ... saw ... and so on. Then as the morning went on we saw variety of animals and birds, and moderate numbers of them. ... look at all those zebras! ... look, a pride of lions! ... and so on. It was already becoming clear that we were in the animals' home - they were free to move about and we were constrained to the tracks and the vehicles. It seemed they did not perceive that there were people to worry about inside the cars.

By late morning, the rest of the other camps' Cruisers had arrived. It is a very large crater, so, mostly it's not an issue. Until something interesting turns up, like lions feeding at a kill. Then the Cruisers converge. But they stay on the track, so it's not as bad as it could be. The guides chatter over the radio, so the density of Cruisers increases the chance of something interesting being spotted and then communicated to other guides.

There is a marsh area of the lake that is home to some hippos. There are picnic tables set up with great viewing while lunch happens. James had packed a fine picnic lunch from the Camp. We left the crater in mid-afternoon to have tea then meet up with a Maasai local guide for what turned out to be one of only two noticeable walks on the trip. We had a great chat and stroll with him through the forest and are now semi-expert on elephant dung. He was dressed in a traditional Maasai blanket, village-made sandals made from tires with leather straps, and a walking stick he had made. We were talking about bird ID and I mentioned the Merlin phone app. He said, "oh, I use that too. I forgot my smartphone, give me yours." and proceeded to type quickly with both thumbs... While we were walking, our guide had agreed to meet up elsewhere on the rim with another guide who had picked up our luggage from Kilimanjaro.

As a measure of how much we saw, I took some 500 photos that first (only) day in the crater. That night I realized I couldn't keep that up, but the photo count dropped to more reasonable numbers for the rest of the trip. It just showed how remarkable Ngorogoro Crater is. Another measure is that we saw 4 of the Big 5 animals that day (number 5 seen the next day at Lake Manyara).

The next morning we were off to Lake Manyara, a 2-3 hr drive from the camp. Similar experience in the Land Cruiser, but there we had close encounters with elephants, one of which we could easily have touched. We refrained.

Then off to Tarangire and our next camp, Sanctuary Swala. It is deep inside the park and takes about 2 hours of game drive to get to. More African massage. Swala was a bit fancier than Lemala, but same concept. The common area was more developed, and they had a watering hole out front with great viewing of its visitors (we saw mostly elephants and zebras). Similar operation - table to ourselves, great personalized service from top to bottom. The outdoor shower at our tent was fun.

In Tarangire, we filled in some of the variety - our first (and then many) giraffes, many elephants, more bird and animal species.

Nairobi was our hub for Kenya and Tanzania. James drove us back to Arusha airport (smaller than Kilimanjaro) for the Cessna flight to Nairobi. We watched our bags go into the underneath pod. Cessnas are small enough that you have to leave backpacks in a heap at the back of the plane. Nice big windows to look out of!

We flew into Wilson airport in Nairobi and stayed at the East Africa Aero Club. It's been maintained as an old time flying club. It was fun wandering the common rooms looking at all the old photos and artifacts. We'd read a book by Beryl Markam about her time as a pilot there in the early to mid 1900s; she was connected to this flying club.

Then a Dash 8 to Maasai Mara. Domestic flights are pretty low key - not much security and the bush camps target is a dirt airstrip. We were met by our guide, Fiona Nashipae, who told us she was our guide for our stay. We'd been told not to get too used to our private tour in Tanzania - the rest of the camps would have shared guides and Cruisers. But we lucked out at Entim and we got Fiona to ourselves. Rinse and repeat for the Camp operation. We were becoming old hands at this.

The Maasai Mara is a great open plain. But practically speaking, it was more African massage. Our Cruiser here had removable roof panels, so we could stand, but there was no shade. And there was dust.

It was a little unsettling that here the drivers did not stick to the tracks, so if something interesting was found, that something was quickly surrounded. It seemed like it must be invasive, but the animals showed no obvious signs of distress. Still. Fiona's take was that the only solution is fewer guests (note that Botswana has taken this approach - lower volume, higher cost).

It didn't take too long to realize that we hadn't noticed any other female drivers, so we asked Fiona about it - she said 10% are females, and they keep in touch. Fiona also does beading (designs and sells), and surprised us by doing the evening drives in a shawl and dress she had beaded along with beaded wrist and neck bands. Deb didn't recognize her at first!

It was here we had hoped to see the masses of zebra and wildebeest on the move. We were not disappointed in that regard. We had not actually been very interested in seeing a crossing, but as we talked to people who'd seen them, we started getting interested. But we'd booked a hot air balloon ride the first morning - and you guessed it, there was a crossing at our camp while we were out. We never did get another shot. Fiona was dead set on us seeing a cheetah, so we got to experience the "hunt" which we were sure would be fruitless, until the jungle drums turned one up right before the end of our last drive.

The sunrise balloon ride was special. Up at 5 to be driven to the launch site and then be bundled into the basket before they finished inflating the balloon. They had us sit on the inside wall of the basket and ended up standing as the balloon took off. Then an hour drifting across the Maasai Mara - as the sun came up - to see lots of wildebeest and zebras, and the odd elephant and Cape Buffalo below us. The landing was a bit exciting as we ended up being dragged a fair distance since we landed at speed.

Kenya is known for Tuskers beer. It was in all the books we read, and advertised a lot. I got some pressure to try it, even though I despise beer. But I thought, ok, I'll try a sip and reject it. Turns out it was almost drinkable! It tastes more like cider; they go very light on the hops, Google says. That said, I didn't have it again, even though I drank about 1/3 of that bottle.

The camps so far had not been full, even though they were high end and it was peak season. We asked, and were told it had been a bad tourism year due to Ebola, the war in Iran and the World Cup. They'd had a lot of cancellations. (we asked again in Botswana - they were not having such issues)

Then back to Nairobi and to Victoria Falls in a 737 the following day. We stayed at the Victoria Falls Hotel, which is definitely not a bush camp. That is described elsewhere.

From Vic Falls, we flew a Cessna 208 Grand Caravan to Kisane in Botswana. A real airport, though when we were there, ours was the only plane. Quick and easy for passport control/immigration! Then another Cessna to the Okavango Delta, and a dirt airstrip. Our Camp (Okavango) has its own strip, but delta water has been abundant the past year and their strip was flooded. We had a 40' boat ride to get to camp.

Similar camp story. But this camp seemed more of a zen retreat, actually a nice respite from the go-go-go game drives. Even down to the dugout canoe (aka mokoro) rides where they said you will likely not see any wildlife - you'll get a relaxing poled outing and see what it might have been like back then. Though we were treated to a massive bull elephant that came by us in the marsh!

This camp was a bit more regimented. We were sung into the camp ("Dumela!"), and activities were more tightly set. Fixed mealtimes, fixed siesta and set activities. But Deb was sick for maybe 36 hours as we arrived, so a rest was what she needed.

Then a flying minivan (GA8 AirVan) to Leroo La Tau. It's tiny but has big (clean) windows and flies low. The view over the delta and then desert was excellent; as mentioned above some of the delta overhead photos verge on modern art. We were a bit taken aback when the pilot pulled out a paper map - and then turned it upside down.

Leroo La Tau is owned by the same company as Camp Okavango and it became clear there is a formula. Same singing, as well as introducing the camp staff at dinnertime and having the chef and sommelier (barkeep) describe what was on offer. Different to Lemala and Swala, these camps do buffet main courses. You lose out on the presentation.

The tracks are different in Botswana - they are thick sand. Akin to driving in deep snow. The Camp is situated along the Boteti river and has remarkable wildlife visiting the opposite bank. Our veranda had a wonderful view, including of a couple of elephant mudholes. They had a great time in there and we could spend any length of time watching them. The camp keeps Cruisers on the park side of the river, so each game drive involved a boat to go downstream a bit and cross. And impressively more dust. We realized that our buffs made excellent dust masks. Most of the viewing was at the river's edge. But I did finally get to see a Kory Bustard, the world's heaviest flying bird. And coincidentally Botswana's national bird.

One example (of many, many) was our guide Grey finding a male lion at a zebra kill. There was word among the guides that it was out there, but only a general idea of where. He drove and drove and drove looking for it. At one point, he gets out, stands on the fender and says "over there". And indeed it was over there, completely hidden by bush. Later he said he saw a slightly different coloration of his mane. Sheesh. We saw this over and over again from all our guides.

And then off to our final, and 6th camp, Linyanti, again by flying minivan. The logistics are pretty well practised: no tickets, just a manifest the pilot checks off. Pilot does everything. The guide arrives 30' ahead of time to clear the strip of animals (there were Cape Buffalo on the strip for our arrival). Then people get off, the pilot takes their bags out of the under-pod, greets us, loads our bags, we all get in and he takes off. 10' turnaround. Well, the pilot did stop to take a leak in the bushes after he let us off this time.

Linyanti is up in the north, bordering Namibia and is a great marsh area, known for large herds of Cape Buffalo and elephants. Again we were treated to water activities in ther marsh pools of hippos and elephants (separately) cavorting. More sandy tracks, more dust. Every day in all the camps ended with a sundowner. Drinks and snacks on a special fold down tray off the grill of the Cruiser. For our last one a family of elephants came lumbering by on their way into the deeper marsh. They came pretty close. As I was making a video of the matriarch coming closer to check us out, I noticed my Cruiser mates had stepped 5' behind me...

Linyanti also had a female guide; she drove our last "game drive" to the airstrip for departure. We talked to her about it, and she said she started in guiding via a government programme to encourage women into the field. Unlike our other guides, she is a city girl from Gabarone (the capitol) and had excellent English. She'll go far! We had talked to the camp manager on the topic - she said women would take over the field.

We stayed in 6 bush camps. We did have to share guides and vehicles in Botswana, but it was ok. Mostly there were not that many guests and we chatted with many of them at meals and in the Cruisers. The staff at all the camps were uniformly friendly and helpful. We muttered about being confined to our tents during dark hours, but there was plenty of evidence for nocturnal visitors - zebras, elephants, lions and, near water, hippos. The hippos sounded pretty close sometimes. And at Linyanti. an elephant stomped the water pipe and no one had water to start the day.

We took two helicopter rides in Botswana - at Leroo La Tau and at Linyanti. As for Victoria Falls, they gave a great overview: of the Leroo La Tau desert and how infrequent to see animals; and the Linyanti marsh and its big herds. These were both open-door (same heli company, it turned out). It was a bit disconcerting at first to look straight down through an open door as it banked, but we got used to it. The pilots stayed on at the camps and we had a chance to talk with them at more length.

We did notice there is a self-drive option for safaris - camping and rent a Land Cruiser. I can't see how either of those would turn out well! There are things that will eat you; and driving those tracks can't be easy. And they're not trained to spot wildlife. Not for us!

We realized that, in general, English is a second language for most of the camps' staff and guides. Possibly third, as they probably speak a tribal dialect at home, and Swahili or Setswana as national language. Then English, so understanding them took more effort than I'd imagined before arriving.

Bugs. We worried about them, got lots of shots, and had most of our clothes sprayed ahead of time. We also kept covered up with long sleeves and pants. In the end, Richard could not identify a single mosquito bite from the trip. Not so for Deb, the mosquito magnet.

The six camps we stayed in were:

  • Lemala Camp - Ngorongoro
  • Sanctuary Swala - Tarangire
  • Entim - Maasai Mara
  • Camp Okavango
  • Leroo La Tau - Botswana
  • Linyanti Bush Camp
And hotels:
  • Crowne Plaza Airport Hotel, Nairobi
  • East Africa Aero Club - Wilson Airport, Nairobi
  • Victoria Falls Hotel

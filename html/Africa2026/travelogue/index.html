We traveled through Tanzania, Kenya, Zimbabwe, and Botswana from 14 August to 4 September 2026 — four countries and, in a real sense, two different kinds of safari.

We went to Africa primarily to see the migration. We wanted to see 10s to 100s of thousands of Wildebeest and zebras on the move. Bonus points for other animals and birds.

Tanzania and Kenya gave us abundance: the Ngorongoro crater's density and variety, the Serengeti–Mara migration, Tarangire's dry-season elephant herds. Botswana was different — a landscape too dry to support that kind of volume, where everything worth seeing goes to the water. No new animals, but seen for very different reasons.

When we plotted out the trip with our travel agent, Tara Gupta of India by Design, we'd focused on where we would see various wildlife. We came up with too many places and Tara worked with Born Free Safaris to pare it down (if a 24 day trip is pared down). It wasn't until we'd experienced the trip that we realized the logical progression that they'd created for us: blow our minds with volume and variety to start, then move on to the niche environments of Victoria Falls and in Botswana. In the end, we covered 453 miles total in the Land Cruisers.

Every region we visited is governed by water: the migration cycle in East Africa following the rains; Victoria Falls and the mighty Zambezi River; and three sites in Botswana all deriving from water from the Okavango River and its delta, which dies in the desert. The delta itself is water-based - islands, no roads, so transport by air or boat. Two rivers emerge from there: the Boteti, a ribbon of water extending into the desert; and the Linyanti flowing northeast, with its marsh system.

Ngongoro Crater in Tanzania is justifiably famous for its density and variety of wildlife. It is a huge crater, some 20 km across with its own lake. It's a self-contained ecosystem where the animals don't need to leave. Spoiler alert, but to emphasize the point, we saw 4 of the Big Five on day one in the crater; and filled in number 5 the next day at Lake Manyara. Wow.

We were fortunate in our timing: the massive herds of wildebeest and zebra were converging on the Mara River in Kenya, and our bush camp was on the river. We got our wish and saw lines of animals for as far as the eye could see.

East Africa is unique in the world for its volume and diversity of mammals. Nowhere else has anything like it. Somehow they survived, and learned to live with hunters. The Maasai only eat their domesticated animals, and (at least) in Botswana, tribes have totem animals they cannot eat. And happily it seems that ecotourism is providing incentive to keep it that way. Some 60% of the migration path is on unprotected land. There are efforts for conservation tourism where grants are given if conservation efforts protect the animals in those open areas.

This was a nature trip, not cultural: our days were taken up in game drives in custom Toyota Land Cruisers out of (fancy) bush camps. More on that later. We spent no time in cities or towns, but did talk at length with our various guides (who also do the driving and spotting!) to get insights into the countries.

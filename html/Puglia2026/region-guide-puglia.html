Terra d'Otranto

The layers, the language, the olives and the water

Compiled to be honest rather than promotional

Before You Read This

A corridor, not a destination

The field companion is about landscape, geology and wildlife — things true for a hundred thousand years. This volume is about the layers of people on top, and about the present, which is different writing. Puglia is having a decade of enormous attention and enormous trouble at the same time: one of the most fashionable destinations in Europe, and a region that has just lost an entire agricultural landscape to a bacterium. Both are true at once, and the tension between them is the story right now.

So this is candid rather than promotional. There is a good deal here about a dead forest of olive trees, a water supply borrowed from another region, a steelworks that has poisoned a city and employs it, and the emptiness of inland villages. None of it will spoil a week of kayaking — but you will drive through several hundred kilometres of it, and some of what you see from the van is unintelligible or simply shocking unless somebody tells you what it is. The four items that date fastest — the Xylella zone boundaries, the water, the Taranto steelworks, and who holds regional office — were checked against current sources as this was compiled, and each is reported with its date attached.

The one thread that ties the volume together

Puglia's history is unusually legible because it is a corridor rather than a destination. It is the closest part of Italy to Greece and the Balkans — seventy-two kilometres at the Strait of Otranto — and it is flat, fertile and lined with harbours. So everything crossing between the eastern and western Mediterranean came through here: Bronze Age Aegean traders, Spartan colonists, Roman legions bound for Greece, Byzantine administrators, Norman adventurers, Ottoman raiders, and in 1991 twenty thousand Albanians on a single freighter.

Nothing here is native and unmixed, and its people know it. The Salento speaks a Romance dialect with a Greek substrate, in villages that were speaking Greek within living memory, above tombs inscribed in a Balkan language nobody can fully read, under a Baroque imported from Spain. The heel of Italy is a threshold, and it has been standing in the doorway for four thousand years.

The name of this volume is the old one because it records something the modern map hides: for most of its history this peninsula faced east. Otranto, not Bari or Naples, was the point of the region — where the crossing to Greece began, and therefore where the wealth, the administration, the religion and the invasions came from. Puglia has been reoriented toward Rome only recently, and by railway.

The Layers

Four thousand years in the doorway

Every stop on your itinerary is a stratigraphy, and the layers separate unusually cleanly here because each one arrived across the same seventy-two kilometres of water.

The Messapians, and a language nobody can read

Iapygian is the ancient umbrella for the pre-Roman peoples of the heel: Daunians in the north, Peucetians around Bari, and in the Salento the Messapians. They were neither Greek nor Italic, which is the first surprising thing about them. Their language, Messapic, is an extinct Paleo-Balkanic tongue preserved in about six hundred inscriptions in an alphabet borrowed from Western Greek, dating from the mid-sixth century BC to at least the second. You will see it twice this week, at Roca Vecchia and at Punta Ristola. It is only partly understood.

Where did they come from? The traditional answer is a migration from Illyria. This is contested, not settled, and the distinction matters because the "Illyrian" label has been put to nationalist use in both Albanian and Italian popular writing. Archaeologically there is real support: substantial trans-Adriatic contact in the second millennium BC. Linguistically the argument is weak, for the awkward reason that Illyrian itself is barely attested — there is no consensus that Messapic should be counted as Illyrian at all. Eric Hamp grouped them as "Messapo-Illyrian" and further, with Albanian, under an "Adriatic Indo-European"; others prefer a looser "Western Paleo-Balkan." The honest version: these people came from somewhere across that water and spoke something related to what was spoken on the far side, and the details are open.

Their cities are worth knowing by name. Rudiae, just south-west of Lecce, settled by the late ninth or early eighth century BC, has two concentric wall circuits running some four kilometres and a Roman amphitheatre; its identification as the birthplace of the poet Ennius is a Renaissance attribution rather than an ancient one. Cavallino, five kilometres from Lecce, was abandoned in the early fifth century BC — a clean archaeological horizon. Vaste has Messapian walls and, better, a twelfth-century frescoed crypt that is the finest Byzantine interior in the southern Salento. And the two ports: Hydruntum, which is Otranto, and Brentesion, Brindisi, whose name is usually derived from a Messapic word for "stag's head," after the shape of its harbour.

The war between the Greeks and the natives, which the natives won

Taras — Taranto — was founded by tradition in 706 BC by Spartans, Sparta's only overseas colony, and became the richest city of Magna Graecia. This is why the Ionian side of the heel is Greek and the Adriatic side Messapian: two different worlds on one peninsula, forty kilometres apart, which is roughly the distance you drive on Day 4.

Relations were chronically hostile, and the Greeks did not always win. Herodotus records the defeat of Taras and Rhegion by the Iapygians around 473 BC as "the greatest slaughter of Greeks" he knew of. Taras thereafter took to hiring mainland generals, with poor results: Archidamus III of Sparta was killed outside Manduria in 338 BC, and finally Pyrrhus fought his famous Italian war on Taras's behalf against Rome, winning the battles and coining a phrase.

Rome, and a road to Greece

The Messapians largely sided with Rome. Taras fell in 272 BC, and a Latin colony was planted at Brundisium in 244 BC, making it the empire's gateway to the East. Everything follows. The Via Appia ended at Brindisi, where two columns marked the terminus — one still stands, and the shaft of the other was given to Lecce in 1666 and topped with a statue of Sant'Oronzo, where it stands in the square you walk through on Day 0. The Via Traiana followed in AD 108–110 through Egnatia, which is why you can put your fingers in Roman cart ruts on Day 5. Virgil died at Brundisium in 19 BC on his way back from Greece; and Cannae, where Hannibal destroyed a Roman army on 2 August 216 BC, is in northern Puglia — the Roman dead given as roughly seventy thousand by Polybius and forty-eight thousand by Livy, which is a useful reminder of how ancient numbers work.

Byzantium: the long Greek period

This is the layer most visitors miss and the one that explains the most. The Byzantines recovered Bari in 876 and made it their Italian capital; the Theme of Longobardia was organised in the late ninth century and later absorbed into the Catepanate of Italy, governed from Bari, and Byzantine rule lasted until 1071. Nearly two centuries, on top of centuries of earlier imperial presence.

The Salento was the deepest and most continuously Greek-administered part — garrisoned and repopulated from the East, with Basilian monasteries as the institutional carriers. That is why it kept the Greek rite for centuries after the Normans arrived, and why, in a pocket of villages inland from Otranto, it kept the Greek language. The visible residue is the rock-hewn churches cut into the walls of the gravine canyons and painted; the most remarkable within reach is Santa Cristina at Carpignano Salentino, carrying a dated inscription of AD 959 naming its painter, Theophylact — among the earliest securely dated Byzantine wall paintings in Italy. One correction: the story that these caves were carved by refugee monks fleeing iconoclasm is folklore, not archaeology.

Normans, Frederick II, and Spain

Robert Guiscard was recognised Duke of Apulia at Melfi in 1059 and took Bari in 1071, ending Byzantine Italy. Bari's Basilica di San Nicola was begun in 1087 to house relics stolen from Myra; it is the prototype of Apulian Romanesque and why Bari is still an Orthodox pilgrimage city. The Otranto mosaic floor you stand on belongs to this moment — laid 1163–65, in a Norman kingdom, by a man who had somehow heard of King Arthur.

Frederick II treated Puglia as his heartland: the Constitutions of Melfi in 1231, castles across the region, and above all Castel del Monte near Andria, whose relentless octagonal geometry has generated a very large literature and no agreement whatever on what it was for — hunting lodge, symbolic crown, astronomical instrument. Report the dispute; do not settle it.

Then Angevins from 1266, Aragonese from 1442, Spanish viceregal rule from 1503. Spanish Puglia faced Ottoman and Barbary corsairs, and the answer was the coastal watchtowers — largely built in the 1530s to 1560s under Charles V, sited in line of sight, of which perhaps ninety survive in the old Terra d'Otranto. Half the coastal place-names on your kayaking route are towers, and the field companion describes how the signalling worked. The event that made them necessary was the sack of Otranto in 1480: an Ottoman fleet landed on 28 July, the city fell on 11 August, and on 14 August the male survivors — traditionally eight hundred and thirteen — were executed on the hill above the town. The bones are behind glass in the cathedral, and the visit distresses some people; worth saying in advance rather than after.

Why Lecce looks like that

The Lecce Baroque is the most extravagant provincial architecture in Italy, with three causes operating at once. First, money — specifically olive money: Terra d'Otranto oil was an export commodity, shipped from Gallipoli to light the streets of northern Europe, and it made the region genuinely wealthy in the seventeenth century. Second, competition: a dense population of religious orders building against one another in a Counter-Reformation climate, with Spanish taste running to horror vacui. And third, the stone. Pietra leccese is soft enough to be carved with a knife when freshly quarried and hardens on exposure to air, which made deeply undercut foliage cheap. When you wonder how anyone afforded a façade in Lecce, part of the answer is that the stone was doing the work. The names: Gabriele Riccardi, who began Santa Croce in 1549; and above all Giuseppe Zimbalo, called lo Zingarello, who did its upper façade and the enormous cathedral bell tower of 1682.

Unification, and the emptying

Italian unification in 1860–61 was not, in the south, an unambiguous liberation: northern tariffs, great estates, near-universal illiteracy, and a peasantry whose hopes of land were disappointed. What followed was brigandage — a rural insurgency as much as banditry, suppressed under an emergency law of 1863 — and then the emigration. Something like fourteen million people left Italy between 1876 and 1915, Puglia joining later and more heavily than Campania, and again in the 1950s to 1970s, northward. Nearly every Salento village has a piazza emptier than its houses, and you will notice it. The questione meridionale was named in the nineteenth century and has not been closed.

1991, and the image that changed how Italy sees itself

In March 1991, as the Albanian state collapsed, something like twenty-five thousand Albanians crossed to Brindisi and Otranto. On 8 August 1991 the freighter Vlora, hijacked at Durrës, docked at Bari with roughly twenty thousand people aboard, packed onto every horizontal surface; they were held in the city's football stadium. The photographs are the founding image of Italy as a country of immigration rather than emigration, which happened within a single generation.

The sequel is on your route. On 28 March 1997 the Albanian vessel Kater i Radës, crowded with migrants, sank off Otranto after a collision with an Italian warship during an interception. At least eighty-one died and probably many more, and the gap between the confirmed and estimated tolls is part of the story. The raised hull is now part of a monument on the Otranto waterfront by Costas Varotsos, L'Approdo — The Landing.

Griko

A Greek language at the end of its life

Inland and north-west of Otranto, in nine villages, people speak a variety of Greek called Griko — one of two Italiot Greek varieties, the other surviving in a smaller pocket in Calabria. Note the geography, because it matters practically: Grecìa Salentina lies inland, so the coast road you take south on Days 2 and 3 does not pass through it. Melpignano, Corigliano d'Otranto and Martano are twenty-five to thirty-five kilometres from Lecce, which makes your free Saturday the opportunity.

The linguistic nine are Calimera, Castrignano dei Greci, Corigliano d'Otranto, Martano, Martignano, Melpignano, Soleto, Sternatia and Zollino. The administrative union later took in three more comuni that are not Greek-speaking, which is why published counts vary.

How many speakers, honestly. Figures here have been inflated by tourist boards for decades. Older surveys claimed some twenty thousand people with competence; realistic recent assessments put fluent native speakers in the low thousands at most, plausibly the hundreds, overwhelmingly over seventy. UNESCO classes Griko as severely endangered, and transmission is effectively broken: almost nobody under fifty acquired it at home.

Where did it come from? A genuinely open question

Gerhard Rohlfs argued for direct continuity from Magna Graecia — an unbroken Doric-descended vernacular spoken here since the Greek colonies, which would make Griko a living relic of the classical world. Giuseppe Morosi and others argued the enclaves are residue of Byzantine-era settlement, seventh to eleventh centuries, and therefore medieval rather than ancient.

The modern middle position is that Byzantine repopulation is the proximate source, possibly settling onto an older Greek substrate, with the enclave's boundaries drawn by medieval rather than classical patterns. Present it as a live dispute. The local formulation you will hear — "we have spoken Greek here since Pericles" — is a claim rather than a finding, and one with a good deal of civic pride invested in it. The Byzantine chapter is the reason the Byzantine answer is probably nearer the truth.

Revitalisation in practice means bilingual signs, elective school courses, a small publishing effort, and festivals — but by far the most effective vector has been music. A great deal of the Notte della Taranta repertoire is in Griko, and two songs in particular, Kalinifta and Aremu rindineddha, are known to hundreds of thousands of Italians who do not speak a word of the language. The honest prospect: Griko will survive as heritage, performance and village pride, and not as a spoken vernacular. Worth stating plainly rather than sentimentally, because the two outcomes need completely different things and only one is still available. Three words to carry anyway, because saying them in the right village produces a genuinely warm response: kalimera, kalinifta, kalò.

The Olives, and What Killed Them

The most important thing to understand before you look out of the window

Puglia is the dominant olive region of Italy — something like fifty to sixty million trees producing about half the national oil in a normal year. And in the decade since 2013, the commercial olive economy of the two southernmost provinces has effectively ended. This is not a footnote. It is the largest change to a European agricultural landscape in living memory, and you will drive through the middle of it for four days. It needs explaining before you see it, because the alternative is bewilderment or the assumption that somebody has been careless.

What happened

Xylella fastidiosa subspecies pauca, sequence type ST53, was first detected in Europe in October 2013 in the Gallipoli and Alezio area — essentially at your Day 4 launch site — on olives showing what growers already called olive quick decline syndrome. The strain is genetically closest to Central American isolates, and the working hypothesis is introduction on ornamental coffee plants. The bacterium colonises the xylem and blocks water transport, so the tree dies from the top down. There is no cure and no field-effective treatment. The vector is the meadow spittlebug, whose young live in ground cover — which is why compulsory spring tillage became part of the control regime, and still applies across dozens of comuni below two hundred metres.

The regime, and why people fought it

The EU response created an infected zone, a buffer zone, and — in the deep Salento, where eradication was abandoned as impossible — a containment area. In the eradication zone, infected plants had to be felled together with host plants within a specified radius.

Compulsory felling of healthy-looking and sometimes monumental trees produced bitter resistance: blockades and tree occupations, a stream of court injunctions, and in 2015 a criminal investigation by the Lecce prosecutor into researchers and officials, eventually shelved without trial. Alongside real grievance ran thick conspiracy theory — that the epidemic was invented to clear land for tourism, golf or photovoltaics; that the gas pipeline or agrochemicals were the cause; that Xylella does not kill olives at all. Some of this is still widely believed. The consensus is the consensus and should be reported as such. But it is worth being fair about why trust collapsed: the state arrived with chainsaws before it arrived with compensation, and it was asking people to cut down trees their great-grandparents planted on the strength of a diagnosis they could not see.

The scale, and where the front is now

The most-repeated figure is around twenty-one million trees dead or affected across the Salento, and it should be used carefully, because it conflates dead, infected and merely symptomatic. Industry estimates put cumulative damage in the range of one and a half billion euros and production losses in Lecce province at eighty to ninety per cent. Lecce and Brindisi provinces are, as a commercial oil economy, post-olive.

As for the front: it has moved north-north-west year on year, and the answer is no longer in doubt. The delimited infected zone now takes in Monopoli, Polignano a Mare, Castellana Grotte, Alberobello and part of Putignano — which is to say that the Valle d'Itria, where you sleep on Days 5 and 6, and Monopoli, where the trip ends, are inside the delimited area rather than beyond it. Two qualifications, both real. The advance has slowed markedly, with monitoring finding the northern limit holding roughly stable and isolated outbreaks further north eradicated as they appeared. And "infected zone" is a legal designation covering a whole comune, not a description of every grove in it: much of the Fasano–Ostuni monumental belt is still visibly alive. Ask your hosts anyway on the first evening — they will know their own trees, and it is the most important fact in their working lives.

What replaces them, and what you will actually see

Only two tolerant cultivars are authorised for replanting: Leccino, which becomes infected but carries a low bacterial load, and FS-17, sold as Favolosa. Tolerant, note, not immune. Replanting is slow — permits, cost, water, five to ten years to production — and many growers have switched to vines, pomegranates or almonds, or sold to solar developers.

Between Lecce and Leuca, and especially between Gallipoli and Alessano, you are in the epicentre, and it is shocking. Kilometre after kilometre of grey-white skeletal trunks, bark peeling, bare in the season when an olive grove should be silver. Whole groves standing dead like a forest that burned without fire. Elsewhere groves felled, with stacked cordwood in the field corners. Monumental trunks sawn to stumps and re-sprouting a mop of green from the base — some Leccino grafts, some just old rootstock trying again, and easy to misread as recovery when it usually is not. Cleared red earth replanted with young vines and almonds in tight modern rows. And an unmistakable spread of solar arrays on former grove land. Also dry-stone walls now enclosing nothing, and a serious fire risk from all that standing dead wood. This is an ex-landscape, and saying so is more respectful than not.

Between the Salento and the Valle d'Itria on Day 5, the transition is legible from the car. Around Ostuni and Fasano the monumental groves are still substantially alive — enormous contorted trunks, silver canopies, dry-stone walls, underground mills — interleaved with dead patches and freshly cleared squares. Higher up, the Valle d'Itria is still green and dense.

Two things to hold in mind while looking at it

The age claims are inflated. The Fasano–Ostuni plain holds Italy's greatest concentration of monumental olive trees, protected by regional law. Trunks of a thousand years and more are defensible. The commonly retailed "two-thousand-year-old Roman tree" is not: olive trunks hollow out and regenerate from the base, so the visible wood is far younger than the organism, and single-tree ages of that order are essentially unverifiable. The groves are genuinely ancient as groves.

And the groves were an industrial plant. The reason the Salento is covered in olives is that seventeenth- to nineteenth-century Europe needed lamp oil, and Gallipoli was the principal export port for it. Kerosene ended that trade a century and a half ago and the trees stayed on, converted to edible oil. What has died here is the last surviving physical form of an obsolete energy industry — which does not make it less of a loss, but explains why there were quite so many trees to lose.

Water, Which Decides Everything

The quiet crisis behind the visible one

Puglia is the driest large region in Italy and has almost no perennial rivers. The geology of that is in the field companion; what follows is what people did about it, and what is happening now.

The defining public work is not a dam but the Acquedotto Pugliese, authorised in 1902, which takes water from the Sele springs at Caposele in Campania — on the far side of the Apennine watershed — and runs it the length of Puglia by gravity. Water first reached Bari on 24 April 1915. It is among the largest single networks in Europe, on the order of twenty thousand kilometres, serving some four million people. But the supplementary sources matter more than the founding story, and they are the region's political vulnerability: Puglia also draws on the Basilicata reservoirs — Monte Cotugno, Pertusillo, Camastra, Basentello. Puglia's dependence on Basilicata's water is the central fact of its water politics, and Basilicata knows it. You meet the aqueduct's ceremonial terminus on Day 2, at the monumental dry waterfall descending to the harbour at Leuca.

2026 has been, by recent standards, a good year — and the argument has moved. 2024 was severely dry across the far south, with states of emergency in Sicily and Basilicata, rotational rationing in Potenza as the Camastra reservoir collapsed, and consequent irrigation cuts to Pugliese consortia. But winter and spring rain left Puglia's reservoirs around 77 per cent full in early summer 2026, and the Basilicata reservoirs at about 81 per cent of authorised volume, some 128 million cubic metres up on the previous year. The complaint now is not drought but leakage and missing infrastructure: the farmers' association reported Puglia losing 12.66 million cubic metres from its reservoirs in a single week in the summer heat, and is pressing for new storage rather than emergency relief.

Salt in the wells

The other half of the story is underground and more serious, because it is not seasonal. The Salento's karst aquifer is a lens of fresh water floating on sea water, and because the water table stands only a metre or three above sea level, the lens is thin — tens of metres. Decades of over-abstraction, including many thousands of unauthorised wells, have thinned it further and drawn the salt interface upward and inland. Chloride levels in coastal and even inland wells have risen enough to damage crops and in places exceed potable limits — and irrigating with brackish water then salinises the soil, compounding it. Treated wastewater has historically been disposed of into karst voids, the injection sink at Nardò being the notorious case, putting effluent directly into the aquifer.

For a reader who likes systems, this is the quiet crisis behind the visible one. Xylella killed the trees. Water scarcity decides whether anything replaces them. A grower choosing between tolerant olives, table grapes and a solar developer is making a water decision as much as an economic one — and the solar array needs none.

And the practical question: can you drink it?

Yes. Puglian tap water is potable and compliant with EU and Italian standards, and Tofino's own packet says so. The widespread local belief otherwise has three roots, none of them safety. History: water has always been imported here, precious, rationed in dry years — and that attitude outlives the engineering. Taste: the water is very hard, being drawn from limestone, and often tastes strongly of chlorine at the far end of long mains, so locals prefer bottled on flavour grounds. And genuine local problems: older coastal networks have suffered saline intrusion, and municipalities do occasionally post acqua non potabile notices.

So: drink it, expect a mineral and chlorine taste, and a carbon filter bottle fixes that. Obey any non potabile sign — common on garden, harbour and cemetery taps. And never drink from a coastal karst spring, however cold and inviting: it is brackish and unprotected.

Who Lives Here Now

Work, steel, electricity, and what you are part of

Puglia has roughly 3.9 million people and the number is falling — the classic southern combination of very low fertility plus persistent net out-migration of the young and educated, with one estimate putting something like a million people leaving the Mezzogiorno between 2002 and 2021, disproportionately graduates. In the inner Salento the effect is visible from the road: villages with beautiful housing stock, immaculate churches, and nobody under forty in the piazza at six o'clock. Unemployment has improved to roughly ten or eleven per cent from the mid-teens a decade ago, but the more telling figures are that youth unemployment remains around or above thirty per cent and the employment rate is near fifty per cent, well below the Italian average. Read together, those numbers describe an economy in which a great many working-age people are neither employed nor counted as unemployed.

Taranto: employment and epidemiology in direct conflict

You will not go to Taranto, but it is the largest single fact in modern Puglia. The steelworks was built by the state in 1965 as the flagship of the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno; it became Europe's biggest single-site mill, some fifteen hundred hectares, twenty thousand jobs at peak — and a public-health catastrophe in the adjacent Tamburi district, with elevated cancer and respiratory rates that have been the subject of study and litigation for decades.

Privatised in 1995; partially seized by prosecutors in 2012 in a landmark emissions prosecution; taken over by ArcelorMittal in 2018; renamed Acciaierie d'Italia in 2021 with the state as co-investor; and placed under extraordinary administration in February 2024. Two binding offers arrived at the December deadline, from Bedrock Industries and the Flacks Group, and in early 2026 the commissioners — authorised by the industry ministry — entered exclusive negotiation with Flacks. The plan on the table runs to roughly €5 billion of investment and about 8,500 jobs against the present 9,741, with the state retaining a 40 per cent stake to be bought out later, and closure targeted for the first months of 2026. Whether it has closed by the time you travel is worth a glance at the news.

What is not in doubt is the structure: Taranto is a city where a very large number of families depend on a plant that is measurably shortening lives, no Italian government of any colour has resolved it, and every proposed solution requires either enormous public money or accepting the loss of the jobs. If anyone raises it, that is the honest frame.

Electricity, and a pipeline under your Day 1 beach

A fact that surprises most visitors: Puglia is Italy's leading wind region and among its top two for solar, and a substantial net exporter of electricity. Installed wind capacity is on the order of three gigawatts — about a quarter of the national total. Beleolico, in Taranto's outer harbour, was Italy's first offshore wind farm, commissioned in April 2022. The politics are not simple: there is real local opposition to consumo di suolo, with utility-scale solar occupying former farmland including land cleared of dead olives. When you see an array where a grove used to be, you are looking at the intersection of the last two chapters.

And the Trans Adriatic Pipeline makes landfall at San Foca, comune di Melendugno — the same few kilometres of coast as Torre dell'Orso and the Grotta della Poesia. It carries Azeri gas, ten billion cubic metres a year, expandable to twenty; the shore crossing is a microtunnel beneath the beach and the receiving terminal sits eight kilometres inland. The No TAP movement of 2015–2018 was one of Italy's bitterest local environmental conflicts: the transplanting of a couple of hundred olive trees, clashes with police in 2017, dozens prosecuted, and — still resented — the Five Star Movement's abandonment of its promise to block the project on entering government. After Russia's gas cuts in 2022, TAP's flows rose and national criticism largely evaporated. And what you see at San Foca is almost nothing: the pipeline is buried, the crossing tunnelled, the beach looks like a beach. The gap between the scale of the fight and the invisibility of the result cuts both ways — the mitigation genuinely worked, and the people who fought it are entitled to say it worked because they fought.

Tourism, and what you are part of

Fifteen years ago Puglia was a region Italians holidayed in. Overnight stays were around fifteen million in 2019 and have set records since, with the foreign share climbing toward forty per cent. The drivers are identifiable: low-cost airlines at Bari and Brindisi; the masseria-hotel model, which converted a distressed agricultural building stock into luxury accommodation almost overnight; sustained international press on the theme of "the new Tuscany"; and the G7 summit held at a resort near Fasano in June 2024, an enormous free advertisement broadcast from a few kilometres from your second masseria.

The pressure points will be visible to you. Alberobello has about ten thousand residents and well over a million visitors a year; Rione Monti is now almost entirely retail and the residents have largely left the trulli. It is fed by coach itineraries and cruise calls, which is why late September improves it much less than you would hope — the fix is the hour, not the month. Polignano a Mare's Lama Monachile is a small pebble cove receiving crowds far beyond capacity. Otranto, by contrast, is transformed: in the last week of September you can park, walk the Aragonese walls at sunset alone, and see a twelfth-century mosaic floor without queuing — perhaps seventy or eighty per cent fewer visitors than August, and the town reverts to being a working port of five and a half thousand people.

Short-term rental growth has been among Italy's fastest, with the predictable effect on local rents, and summer peaks strain the aqueduct and waste systems of villages that multiply tenfold in August — which connects directly to the water chapter. And one reliably explosive small subject if it arises: the lidi that occupy most of the accessible coastline hold long-standing state concessions which European rules oblige Italy to put out to competitive tender, a reform successive governments have delayed for years. It is why the empty beach you land on in late September belongs, formally, to somebody.

The national and regional frame, briefly

At the time of writing Italy's head of state is Sergio Mattarella, re-elected in January 2022, and the government is led by Giorgia Meloni, in office since October 2022, with a general election due by 2027. Growth is weak but positive, inflation near two per cent, public debt around a hundred and thirty-five per cent of GDP. The dominant fiscal story of 2026 is the post-pandemic recovery programme — Italy's national plan under NextGenerationEU, roughly €194 billion, whose spending deadline falls in mid-2026, which is why there is scaffolding on so many public buildings.

Regionally, Puglia's long Emiliano era ended with the 2025 election. Antonio Decaro — formerly mayor of Bari, then an MEP — was proclaimed president on 7 January 2026, presented a ten-member cabinet on the 16th, and the twelfth legislature opened on 2 February 2026 with Toni Matarrelli as president of the regional council. If Puglia comes up in conversation, Decaro is a Bari figure rather than a Salento one, and that distinction still matters here.

Courtesies That Travel

Language, churches, cameras and two conversations

The dining and coffee conventions are in the field companion. What follows is everything else — and the one habit worth adopting above all others is the greeting. Buongiorno until mid-afternoon, buonasera from late afternoon, and southerners switch early. Salve is a useful neutral fallback; ciao is for people you know, and with a stranger it reads as presumptuous. Say buongiorno on entering a shop, bar or waiting room and arrivederci on leaving. Walking in and out silently is the commonest tourist rudeness in Italy and it costs nothing to fix. Also useful: permesso? on entering someone's space, scusi rather than scusa, and prego, which does the work of "please do," "after you," "you're welcome" and "what can I get you."

What you are actually hearing

Salentino is a distinct Romance dialect, not accented Italian. Older villagers may genuinely be more comfortable in it, and a smiling non ho capito, scusi is all that is needed. Its audible signature is a retroflex ddh, so bello becomes beddhu. Words you will hear repeatedly: sciamu! (let's go), mo' (now), beddhu (beautiful), cce ete? (what is it?), nu picca (a little), cumpà (mate), spicciato (finished), and sponzare, to soak — which is what you do to a frisella. And pizzicare, to pinch, the root of pizzica.

Churches, custodians and cameras

Shoulders and knees covered, for both sexes, hats off for men — enforced, sometimes brusquely, at Otranto cathedral, San Nicola in Bari and Santa Croce in Lecce, all of which staff the door. Carry a light scarf and you will never be turned away. Do not wander or photograph during mass, and at Otranto do not step onto the roped sections of the mosaic. A practical point that catches people out: many of the best things in the Salento need a custodian and open only at set hours or by arrangement — the frescoed crypts at Vaste and Carpignano, the underground olive mills at Gallipoli. Check locally the day before, expect a small cash fee, and be prepared for the arrangement to be somebody's cousin with a key.

No flash in frescoed crypts — prohibited, and it does real cumulative damage to pigment. Ask before photographing people: the fishermen at Gallipoli's market, residents in doorways, the butchers at a Cisternino fornello. Most say yes if asked and resent it if not, and the difference in what you get is enormous. In Alberobello's Rione Monti the trullo interiors are private homes and shops; some owners invite you in and then expect a purchase or a tip, a known friction point, so decide how you feel before crossing the threshold. Do not photograph naval installations at Taranto or restricted areas of Brindisi port.

Money and tipping — the truth rather than the myth

Cards are near-universally accepted, including in small towns; merchants have been legally required to accept them for several years and contactless is standard. But carry a hundred or two in cash for informal parking attendants, sagra stalls, village bars that grumble at putting a €1.20 espresso on plastic, church donations, custodians with keys, and the cash-only fornelli at Cisternino. Prefer bank-branded cash machines to standalone tourist ones, and always decline the machine's own currency conversion.

Italy has no tipping culture in the American sense. Staff are salaried, the coperto is a legal cover charge and not a gratuity, and no percentage is expected. Italians round up, or leave a few euros after a good meal — five to ten on a good dinner for four is generous. Ten per cent is generous by local standards; twenty is not "polite," it is conspicuous. The one exception is the travel industry's own convention: guides and drivers on a guided week are customarily tipped, the going international norm being of the order of five to ten euros per person per day for a lead guide, usually collected as a group at the end. That is a tourism norm rather than an Italian one, and you should know which of the two you are following so you can decide rather than feel obliged.

Two conversations to handle with care

Italy is not a country where politics is dangerous to discuss, and people will tell you what they think with enthusiasm and at length. But two subjects here are not abstractions to the people you are talking to.

The first is Xylella. For a grower or a mill owner or their children, this is not an interesting ecological event; it is the destruction of a family asset and a working life, sometimes accompanied by a court order to cut down the trees themselves. Ask what happened and listen. Do not adjudicate the conspiracy theories out loud, and do not be surprised if the person telling you is still angry with the regional government, the European Union and the scientists in roughly equal measure.

The second is emigration. Ask almost anyone in an inland Salento village about their children and you will be told where they live, which will not be here. It is the most ordinary conversation in the region and also the saddest one, and it is generally offered rather than resented — but it is worth understanding that "beautiful, and so quiet" is not, to a local, a compliment.

How to Use This

And why the mask matters more than the binoculars

This follows the blue What to watch for boxes in the field companion, one section each, with two extra sections at the back for things you will see constantly and never learn the name of. It is deliberately not a picture book. Nobody identifies an animal from a photograph; you identify it from two or three marks, a posture and a behaviour, which is what each entry gives you. A photograph of a rainbow wrasse tells you less than a cigar-shaped fish with a pointed snout and an orange zigzag down the flank that patrols ceaselessly and follows you to steal whatever you disturb.

Every name links to an iNaturalist search, which covers fish, plants and invertebrates as well as birds, and will give you photographs on a phone or tablet. They are search links rather than deep links, so an ambiguous name returns a short list rather than a dead end. The boxes are for ticking.

★ marks the prizes — the things worth asking a guide about by name, or worth a detour. ⚑ marks endemics and near-endemics, which are prizes of a different kind: this is the only sort of place you can see them. ⚠ marks the small number of things that can actually hurt you.

The one piece of equipment advice

On this trip the mask and snorkel are what binoculars are on safari. A great deal of what is in this book lives in the top three metres of water, in fifteen to thirty metres of horizontal visibility, and is completely invisible from above the surface. A cheap mask you can put your face into over the side of the boat converts an empty blue plane into a populated landscape. If you bring one optical instrument, bring that.

Bring binoculars too — eight by thirty-two is ample, and a compact pair you will actually carry beats a superb pair left in the van. The seabirds are horizon birds and the migrants are distant.

Where this book is honest instead of encouraging

A guide that lists everything recorded in a region is useless, because it does not tell you what to expect. So a number of entries here exist specifically to say you have missed this, and here is why — the lesser kestrel and the roller, gone by mid-September; the scops owl, silent and departed; the sea daffodil, finished flowering; every orchid but one. A few say this is effectively gone from the sea: the fan mussel above all. And several say this needs a boat, not a kayak.

That is deliberate. Late September in Salento is a genuinely rich moment — the best snorkelling water of the year, the peak of autumn passerine passage, loggerhead hatching, and an entire flora of leafless autumn bulbs — but it is rich in specific things, and knowing which ones is the difference between a good week of looking and a frustrated one.

One note on how this was checked. Where a claim is the sort that a single new record or a changed regulation would overturn, it has been verified against current sources — and in one case that verification changed the entry substantially. The viper section originally told you there are no vipers in the Salento. There are, and the recorded localities are on your route. It is worth reading.

The Meadow and the Shallows

Days 1, 4 and 6 · 27 & 30 September, 2 October

The turn of September into October is the best snorkelling week of the Salento year: the sea is at its annual peak temperature, the water is settled, the warm-water species are at maximum abundance, and the summer's juveniles are still in the shallows. Everything below is realistically within five metres of the surface, and most of it within two.

Seagrass and algae

  • ☐Neptune grass ⚑ Posidonia oceanica — A flowering plant, not an alga, and endemic to the Mediterranean. Ribbon leaves about a centimetre wide with a blunt tip, in bundles of five to eight from a woody rhizome; the clincher against look-alikes is leaf width plus the brown fibrous sheath on the rhizome. It extends one to six centimetres a year, which makes it one of the slowest-growing plants known, and single clones spread over kilometres. From the surface: dark, tall, textured, sharp-edged, standing on a raised step.
  • ☐Dead matte — Not a species but the most important thing to be able to recognise. Beneath a living meadow is the matte: a felted terrace of interwoven rhizomes and trapped sediment, metres thick and centuries to millennia old. When the shoots die the matte remains. Look for a flat-topped step of bare grey-brown substrate with a sharp vertical edge and no green on it, sometimes with cut rhizome ends visible in the wall like a peat cutting. Living meadow has texture and motion; dead matte is smooth, static and slightly sunken. Shoots can be replanted; matte cannot be rebuilt on a human timescale.
  • ☐Little Neptune grass Cymodocea nodosa — The look-alike. Leaves only two to four millimetres wide, two to four per shoot, with finely serrated tips, and a thin rhizome with ring-like leaf scars and no brown sheath. Occupies shallower, sheltered, sandier ground and forms pale, sparse, low stands on flat sand.
  • ☐Peacock's tail Padina pavonica — Small pale fan- or funnel-shaped calcified blades with concentric whitish bands, in clumps in warm shallow pools and on the first metre of shelf. Calcified, so it feels crisp and chalky. An indicator of warm, sheltered, well-lit shallows.
  • ☐Invasive caulerpa Caulerpa cylindracea — Light-green feathery fronds with flat, oval, slightly upturned "grape" branchlets, smothering rock and dead matte in a continuous carpet. One of the more consequential Mediterranean invasions, and easy to identify once you have seen it once.
  • ☐Aegagropiles — "sea balls" — A strandline object rather than an organism. Fibrous felted grey-brown balls, marble to fist size and sometimes near-perfect spheres, made of lignified rhizome-sheath fibre stripped and rolled by wave action in sandy hollows. The same fibre — "Neptune's beard" — was harvested historically as mattress stuffing, packing and insulation.
  • ☐Banquettes — The terraced brown berms of shed Posidonia leaves, sometimes over a metre high, layered like puff pastry and surprisingly solid to walk on. They absorb winter wave energy, hold a large fraction of the beach's sand inside the litter, and feed the sandhoppers and beetles the strandline birds are eating. Mechanically clearing them removes the sand permanently and accelerates the erosion the clearing was meant to prevent. A beach with an intact banquette in late September is a well-managed beach.

Wrasses and damselfish

  • ☐Ornate wrasse Thalassoma pavo — Green-turquoise crossed by narrow vertical orange-red bars; males add a blue head cut off from the body by a broad orange saddle, and a crescent-moon tail with drawn-out lobes. Nervous, restless, fast, hovering just above warm rock in the top three metres and never still. A thermophilic species whose spread northward is one of the cleanest signals of Mediterranean warming.
  • ☐Rainbow wrasse Coris julis — Cigar-shaped with a pointed snout, and two utterly different looks that are the same species: the female phase is brown-red above and white below with a straight dark line; the secondary male is green-blue with a bright orange zigzag stripe, a black spot at the pectoral base and a yellow crescent behind the head. It is protogynous — most individuals begin female and later transform — so this is the commonest identification trap on the route. Patrols ceaselessly, follows a snorkeller to snatch what you disturb, and buries itself in sand to sleep.
  • ☐Damselfish Chromis chromis — Oval, deeply forked tail, big eye; adults sooty brown-black, and the juveniles brilliant iridescent electric blue. Late September is peak blue-juvenile season. Hangs in midwater shoals a metre or two above rock, plankton-feeding, and "rains" back down onto the reef the instant a shadow crosses.

Seabreams and grazers

  • ☐Salema Sarpa salpa — Silvery with about ten or eleven golden-yellow horizontal lines, a small mouth, a blunt sheep-like head and a dark spot at the pectoral base. The only significant herbivorous fish here: it grazes Posidonia leaves in tight, slow, synchronised shoals drifting over the meadow, all heads into the current, often in knee-deep water. On the famous hallucinogenic story: it is real, published, and vanishingly rare — a small number of case reports of temporary hallucinations attributed to indole alkaloids acquired from ingested algae and concentrated in the viscera and head. Not a Salento phenomenon, and associated with eating the guts.
  • ☐White seabream Diplodus sargus — High-backed silver with eight or nine narrow dark vertical bars of alternating strength and a dark blotch on the upper tail base; pointed snout, protruding incisors.
  • ☐Two-banded seabream Diplodus vulgaris — The easy one: exactly two conspicuous black bands, one across the nape behind the gill cover and one round the tail base, on a brassy body with faint gold lines. Juveniles swarm the shallowest wave-washed rock in September.
  • ☐Annular seabream Diplodus annularis — Small, with a black ring on the tail base and — the mark — yellow pelvic fins. Common over meadow.
  • ☐Saddled seabream Oblada melanura — Slim, plain silver, no bars at all, with a single black spot ringed in white at the tail base. An otherwise featureless silver fish with one tail blotch, shoaling in midwater above rock, usually mixed in with damselfish.
  • ☐European sea bass Dicentrarchus labrax — Elongate silver with two clearly separated dorsal fins, a pointed head and a straight back. The giveaway is habitat: solitary or in small groups, hunting inside the white water of the surge on rocky shores, and at harbour mouths. Look into the wave wash you are paddling past.
  • ☐Grey mullets Chelon, Mugil spp. — Take the genus marks first: blunt rounded head, small terminal mouth, two widely separated dorsals, big silvery scales, and the behaviour of skimming the surface film in harbours and vacuuming sediment in visible puffs. Then, if you care: thick-lipped (deep papillose upper lip), flathead (broad head, fatty eyelid), golden (a gold blotch on the gill cover). Guaranteed on the harbour walls at Otranto, Gallipoli and Monopoli.

Bottom-dwellers of sand and mixed ground

  • ☐Striped red mullet Mullus surmuletus — Pink-red with a dark reddish stripe from eye to tail plus two or three yellow stripes, a steep blunt forehead, two chin barbels, and a banded first dorsal. Probes the sand with the barbels and is usually shadowed by an opportunist wrasse stealing what it flushes — a whole food web in one glance. Distinguish from the plain, unstriped red mullet with a near-vertical forehead.
  • ☐Painted comber Serranus scriba — Pale with five to seven dark vertical bars, blue-and-red "scribbled" markings on the cheek and snout, and a violet-blue wash on the belly. Props itself on its pectoral fins on the bottom, moves in short hops, and is inquisitive. A simultaneous hermaphrodite — functionally male and female at once.
  • ☐Common octopus ★ Octopus vulgaris — Find the midden first: a neat scatter of broken bivalve shells and crab carapaces at a hole mouth. Then two eyes on raised turrets with a horizontal slit pupil, and skin that changes texture as well as colour. Compare the white-spotted octopus: slender, reddish with scattered white spots and a conspicuously elongated first arm pair, over sand at dusk.
  • ☐Common cuttlefish Sepia officinalis — Broad flattened body with a fin skirt running the full length of both sides, a W-shaped pupil, and a "zebra" display of dark transverse bands. Honest seasonal note: adults mostly die after spring spawning, so expect small juveniles hovering over sand — plus the reliable evidence, cuttlebones on every strandline.
  • ☐European squid Loligo vulgaris — Torpedo-shaped, translucent flecked with red-brown, with rhomboid fins confined to the rear half of the body. Realistically a night-at-the-harbour-lights animal at Otranto or Gallipoli rather than a daytime sighting.
  • ☐Fan mussel ★ ⚑ Pinna nobilis — You will see the ghost, not the animal. The Mediterranean's largest bivalve, to well over ninety centimetres, endemic, standing vertically in sand and seagrass, anchored by golden byssus threads and usually fouled so that it reads as a fringed brown flap. From autumn 2016 a mass mortality driven mainly by the parasite Haplosporidium pinnae swept the whole basin in three or four years, with site mortality of eighty-five to a hundred per cent; the species is now functionally extinct across most of its range and was reassessed as Critically Endangered in 2019. What remains in Salento meadows is empty valves still standing upright and sediment-filled, or fallen and half-buried — thin, brittle, iridescent inside. But not quite hopeless. Survivors have been found and are being genetically studied in the northern Adriatic under the LIFE PINNA project, which has also carried out the Adriatic's first restocking; and closer to your route, a citizen-science scheme run from the CNR's Taranto laboratory — SOS Pinna: la Subacquea aiuta la Ricerca — has collected reports of a small number of survivors around Taranto, animals that may carry natural resistance to the parasite. So on the Ionian side a live one is not impossible. If you find one — tissue visible in the gape, valves closing as your shadow passes — do not touch it, photograph it, record position and depth, and report it. Single records now matter, and there is a scheme that wants them.
  • ☐Rough pen shell Pinna rudis — The look-alike, and the useful half of the entry. Smaller, to about half a metre, with strong radial ribs bearing rows of large open tubular scales, and it lives on rock and coralligenous bottoms rather than seagrass on sand. It was far less affected by the die-off. So: ribbed and spiny on rock, possibly alive; smooth fan in seagrass, almost certainly a dead shell.
Reading the meadow from the surface — the four signatures

Dark, textured, tall, on a raised step → living Posidonia on living matte.

Pale, sparse, low, on flat sand → Cymodocea.

Smooth flat grey terrace, cut edge, no shoots → dead matte. Centuries of growth, gone.

Brilliant white patch, or a long straight white scar inside dark meadow → anchor damage, which you can count and photograph as you paddle. Anchoring on Posidonia is prohibited under Italian law.

Nobody tells visitors that they can survey the condition of a protected habitat from a kayak with a snorkel mask. You can, and it takes no skill beyond these four lines.

The Rock, the Cave and the Waterline

Days 2 and 3 · 28–29 September · the limestone cliff coast

South of Otranto the shelf narrows and the rock becomes the habitat. This is not meadow country: it is vertical limestone with deep water at its foot, coralligenous overhangs, and caves. The animals change accordingly — cryptic, shade-loving, cave-mouth species, and a set of things built onto the rock itself. It is also where the small number of genuinely painful mistakes are available, so the hazards are in this chapter.

Cave mouths and overhangs

  • ☐Cardinalfish ★ Apogon imberbis — The signature fish of this coast. Small, bright orange-red, with a very large black eye and two separate dorsal fins. Hangs oblique or upside-down in the dark of a cave roof or a deep crevice in small groups, and the eyes throw a torch beam back like coals. The male is a mouthbrooder, carrying the egg mass in his jaws. Look in every cave mouth from Otranto to Leuca.
  • ☐Brown meagre ★ Sciaena umbra — High-backed and deep-bodied, dark bronze-brown with a metallic coppery sheen, blackish fins, and the best mark: brilliant white leading edges to the pelvic and anal fins. No chin barbels. Hovers nose-slightly-down in loose groups in the shade of a cave entrance or a deep overhang, barely moving; the bronze flank flashes when it turns. It drums, vibrating sonic muscles against the swim bladder — loudest at dusk in the spawning season, which is over by now, but individual knocks are still produced and carry through a hull. Put an ear to the deck near a cave mouth at dusk. Protected in Italian spearfishing rules.
  • ☐Dusky grouper ★ Epinephelus marginatus — Heavy-bodied with a huge mouth and big pectorals, brown with irregular pale blotches it can switch on and off, and — diagnostic — a rounded tail with a distinct pale margin. Sits motionless in a cave mouth facing out, then either melts into the dark or, in a properly protected area, swims out to inspect you — and the tameness is itself the sign that you are in a real reserve. Protogynous: the biggest fish are males. Italy's long-standing prohibition on spearfishing grouper plus marine-reserve enforcement has produced marked recovery, with Torre Guaceto one of the most-cited Italian successes. On the open coast, expect a brief distant glimpse.
  • ☐Mediterranean moray ★ Muraena helena — Thick snake-like body, dark brown with yellowish marbling, no pectoral fins, a continuous fin ridge, and a single round gill hole. Head projecting from a hole with the mouth gaping rhythmically — which is breathing, not aggression. Bites happen when a hand goes into a hole, and only then. Compare the conger: plain grey-brown, pectoral fins present, much larger.
  • ☐Black scorpionfish ⚠ Scorpaena porcus — Dark mottled, with a long ragged skin tentacle above each eye. Its relatives: the red scorpionfish is big and red with many fleshy flaps under the chin, and the small red one has a dark blotch on the spiny dorsal. All share the behaviour — absolute immobility, ambush, and total confidence in camouflage, letting you come to a hand's width. Venomous dorsal spines: the risk is handling or putting a hand down, never the fish approaching you.

Built onto the rock

  • ☐Trottoir ★ ⚑ Lithophyllum byssoides — One of the best sights on the route and almost never pointed out. An encrusting coralline red alga that builds a hard horizontal rim, cornice or pavement precisely at mean sea level on exposed limestone, projecting twenty to fifty centimetres and sometimes overhanging into a visor. A sharply defined pink-to-grey-white ledge exactly at the waterline, with a pitted, honeycombed, extremely abrasive surface, best developed on wave-exposed shaded faces. It grows on the order of a millimetre a year, which makes a living trottoir decades to centuries old — and makes a fossil trottoir stranded above present sea level a palaeo-sea-level archive you can photograph. It will also shred hands and gel-coat.
  • ☐Cystoseira / Gongolaria ⚑ — Bushy brown "underwater trees" twenty to sixty centimetres tall, forming a canopy on clean exposed rock at half a metre to three metres, swaying visibly in the surge. Much of the genus has been split into Gongolaria and Ericaria in recent taxonomy, so older books and newer ones disagree. Ecologically these are the Mediterranean's kelp-forest analogue, and they are highly sensitive to pollution, sedimentation and urchin grazing — so finding a healthy canopy is a strong statement about that stretch of coast. Losing it produces an urchin barren.
  • ☐Purple sea urchin ⚠ Paracentrotus lividus — Dark purple to olive-brown with long stout spines, typically wedged into a self-excavated pit and holding shell fragments, pebbles and weed over itself like a hat. That debris-carrying habit is the field mark. Abundant from nought to five metres, and wedged in every crevice at exactly the depth you put a foot down. Do not collect, and do not order them. Stocks were severely depleted for the ricci trade; a 2023 regional law closed the harvest, and in 2026 Puglia extended that closure to 30 June 2029 with monitoring, traceability and only limited licensed experimental takes. Salento monitoring in 2025 found both numbers and diameters recovering on 2023. Urchins on a restaurant menu during your week are illegal.
  • ☐Black sea urchin Arbacia lixula — The pair to the last. Jet black, shorter finer spines, never carries debris, and it sits on bare exposed vertical rock in the surge grazing crustose algae. Its overgrazing helps create the barrens where the Cystoseira canopy has been lost. Colour plus debris habit plus habitat separates them instantly.
  • ☐Marbled rock crab Pachygrapsus marmoratus — The defining animal of this coast. Squarish dark olive-brown carapace about three centimetres across with fine pale yellow marbling, and long legs. Sits out of the water in the splash zone grazing algae, and vanishes sideways into a crack the moment a kayak drifts within two metres. You will see thousands.
  • ☐Hermit crab Clibanarius erythropus — The intertidal one, everywhere in Salento rock pools: blue-grey body with red legs and, diagnostically, two claws of equal size, usually in a topshell. Pagurus species have one markedly enlarged claw. Equal claws does most of the work.

The three things not to touch

  • ☐Bearded fireworm ⚠ Hermodice carunculata — Ten to thirty centimetres, flattened and segmented, orange-red to olive-green, with a conspicuous white bristle bundle on each side of every segment and a pale fleshy caruncle on the head. It crawls slowly and openly across rock in broad daylight, which is exactly what catches people out, because it looks handleable. The calcareous bristles are hollow, detach on contact and drive into skin, producing intense burning for hours to days plus numbness. Never touch it and never brush or rub — that drives them deeper; lift them out with adhesive tape. Increasing rapidly with warming. Directly relevant, because you will be hauling boats over rock and lifting stones all week.
  • ☐Weeverfish ⚠ Trachinus / Echiichthys spp. — A sandy-shallows animal rather than a rock one, and included because standing on one is genuinely agonising. Buried in sand with only the eyes and the tip of the black venomous first dorsal fin exposed. Treatment is hot-water immersion at forty to forty-five degrees. The reason for stiff-soled shoes on the Ionian side as well as the Adriatic.
  • ☐Banded centipede ⚠ Scolopendra cingulata — Not marine, but this is where the "hands into places you cannot see" rule earns its place. Up to fifteen centimetres, banded yellow-and-dark, fast, under stones and in dry-stone walls. It bites, painfully. The general rule for the whole week: no hands blindly into crevices, under overhangs, or into walls.
And the cave animals you will not see, which are why the Zinzulusa is famous

Typhlocaris salentina — a blind, unpigmented shrimp in the anchialine pool at the back of the Zinzulusa. Its only close relatives live in groundwater in Israel and in Cyrenaica. Blind cave shrimps cannot cross open sea, so the distribution is a Tethyan relict: the animals did not travel, the coastline did.

Spelaeomysis bottazzii — a blind stygobitic mysid crustacean endemic to Apulian groundwaters, in the same cave and in wells across the Salento.

Higginsia ciccaresei — a cave sponge described from this pool in the late 1990s. A new animal, from a ticketed Italian show cave, within living memory.

You will not see any of them on a walking tour, and you cannot paddle to them. They are here because they are the best single argument on this trip that biology is a record of geology.

Drifters and Stingers

Peak gelatinous season, and only one of them matters

Late September in the southern Adriatic and the Ionian is a high-biomass period for gelatinous plankton, but composition matters far more than abundance — and the happy arithmetic is that the two you will see most of are the two that barely sting, while the one that matters arrives on the wind rather than on the calendar.

  • ☐Mauve stinger ⚠ Pelagia noctiluca — The real hazard, and it is small. Bell only three to ten centimetres, mushroom-shaped, pink to mauve or lilac, with a warty, granular bell surface, eight long thin marginal tentacles and four long frilly oral arms. Critically, it has stinging cells on the bell as well as the tentacles, so there is no safe part of it and glancing contact stings. Strongly bioluminescent — a blue-green flash if disturbed at night. It is entirely oceanic in its life cycle, with no bottom-living polyp stage, which is precisely why swarms appear overnight with no local warning, delivered inshore by wind and current. Autumn swarms in these waters are entirely normal. The sting is an immediate intense burning with linear whip-like weals, pain for hours, and itching and hyperpigmentation for weeks. Practical rule: if you see three or four in a paddle, expect many more and think twice about swimming.
  • ☐Barrel jellyfish Rhizostoma pulmo — Big and harmless, which is worth knowing in advance because the size is alarming. Bell thirty to sixty centimetres and more, solid, firm and whitish-blue, with a conspicuous violet-blue frilled band round the margin and no long tentacles at all — instead a dense cauliflower-like mass of eight fused oral arms hanging beneath. Sting mild to negligible on ordinary skin; can irritate lips and eyes. Extremely abundant in the Adriatic in autumn, sometimes in hundreds. You will paddle through them.
  • ☐Fried-egg jellyfish Cotylorhiza tuberculata — Unmistakable and at its annual peak on your dates: a smooth domed brownish-yellow central "yolk" in a flatter translucent rim, with clusters of short club-shaped appendages beneath, tipped with white-violet buttons. Almost always with small fish sheltering underneath, which is the clinching behaviour. Sting negligible; routinely handled by local swimmers. The safe jellyfish, and you may see hundreds in a day.
  • ☐By-the-wind sailor Velella velella — Not a jellyfish but a colonial hydrozoan. Two to seven centimetres, a flat oval deep-blue float with a transparent diagonal triangular sail set across it, which is what makes it sail downwind and what strands it in millions. Sting negligible. Mass strandings here are mainly late winter and spring, so on your dates expect the dried "cellophane" sails crackling on the strandline rather than live animals — which makes it a strandline object worth knowing.
  • ☐Portuguese man o' war ⚠ Physalia physalis — Extremely unlikely here, and included only so that you can rule it out. An Atlantic species entering the Mediterranean via Gibraltar, with records clustering in the western basin; Adriatic records are a handful. But learn the one line that separates it from Velella: a "Velella" ten to thirty centimetres long, with a big pink-and-blue crest along the top of the float and threads trailing metres beneath, is not a Velella. Get out of the water and warn people — including on the strandline, where a stranded one stings for days.
  • ☐Mediterranean box jellyfish ⚠ Carybdea marsupialis — Small: bell one and a half to four centimetres, transparent and distinctly cube-shaped, with one long tentacle at each of the four lower corners. Nocturnal, moving into very shallow sandy water at night and strongly attracted to lights. A Mediterranean native, recorded in the Adriatic. The sting is genuinely painful, with linear weals, but this is not in the lethal Indo-Pacific class. It matters only if you swim at dusk or at night off a sandy beach, or under harbour lights — so don't.
  • ☐Sea walnut Mnemiopsis leidyi — "The one you can hold." A transparent lobate ctenophore, five to ten centimetres, oval with two large trailing lobes and eight rows of comb plates that flicker with rainbow iridescence in sunlight. It has no stinging cells whatsoever and is completely harmless to touch — an excellent teaching animal. Native to the western Atlantic, it wrecked the Black Sea anchovy fishery in the 1980s and has bloomed massively in the Adriatic since around 2016, eating fish eggs and larvae. Bloom season is late summer to autumn, so a soupy layer you can scoop with a hand is entirely plausible on your dates.
The three things to stop doing about jellyfish stings

Vinegar is not a general treatment. Acetic acid is specific to Indo-Pacific box jellyfish, where it inhibits the discharge of unfired capsules. On Mediterranean species — Pelagia above all — it has been shown to trigger discharge in some species, making the sting worse.

Urine does nothing. Not sterile, wrong and variable osmolarity, and being hypotonic it can fire unfired capsules. Folklore, and it makes things worse.

Fresh water is actively harmful, for the same osmotic reason; so is alcohol; so is rubbing with a towel or sand, because mechanical pressure discharges the remaining capsules.

Do this instead: out of the water; rinse copiously with seawater; lift adherent fragments off without pressure using the edge of a card or tweezers; then analgesia — hot immersion at about forty-two to forty-five degrees has the best trial evidence, a cold pack in a dry bag is the standard beach measure and also works, and this is the single most disputed point in the field; then a topical steroid and an oral antihistamine, and expect delayed flares at the site for one to three weeks. Seek help for a large-area sting, anything on the face, or any systemic symptom.

And the ironic reversal: for sea urchin spines, vinegar genuinely does help, because acetic acid dissolves calcareous fragments. Soak in hot water for half an hour to an hour, lift out what protrudes, and do not dig or cut. The purple pigment tattoo left behind is harmless. A spine near a joint or in the sole of the foot, or any spreading redness, needs a doctor.

Turtles, Whales and Two Absences

What is realistic from a kayak, and what has gone

The honest framing first. The inshore water you will be paddling is five to forty metres deep, and almost every Mediterranean cetacean except one is a deep-water animal needing the shelf edge, the Otranto Channel or the Gulf of Taranto canyons. A kayak's silence and low profile genuinely help at close range, but you cannot cover water, and marine-mammal spotting is a function of area searched. The realistic cetacean list from a kayak is one species long. The turtle list, by contrast, is much better than you would expect.

Turtles

  • ☐Loggerhead turtle ★ Caretta caretta — Disproportionately large, broad, blocky head with powerful jaws; five pairs of costal scutes on the carapace and two pairs of prefrontal scales between the eyes; reddish-brown, often encrusted with barnacles and trailing algae. What you actually get from a kayak is a fist-sized brown head appearing without warning at fifty to two hundred metres, an audible exhalation, a low brown shell-back, and a slow submergence — often only once. They also rest motionless at the surface in calm water, sometimes with a gull standing on them, and a resting turtle will let a silent kayak approach far closer than a motorboat.
  • ☐A loggerhead nest ★ — Genuinely plannable on your dates, and worth asking about. The Ionian arc is the nesting coast — the sandy beaches from Gallipoli north-west through Punta Prosciutto and Torre Colimena. Nesting runs May to August, incubation takes forty-five to seventy days, so hatching runs late July into October and you are in the tail of it. What you might find: a fenced and signed nest with a volunteer night watch; a hatchling emergence, at night or just before dawn; or a nest excavation a few days after emergence, when volunteers open the chamber to count shells and rescue stragglers — done in the open, and volunteers are usually delighted to explain. Italian loggerhead nesting has climbed from 443 nests in 2023 to 601 in 2024 and over 700 in 2025, a record; Puglia alone had about a hundred nests in 2025, mostly in Lecce province, with hatching concentrated in the Salento and at Porto Cesareo. Coordinated through Legambiente's LIFE Turtlenest. One of the real Mediterranean conservation successes. Rules: never dig; no white light on a nesting beach; never point a torch at a hatchling, since light disorientation is the main cause of hatchling death; report a fresh crawl rather than following it. Strandings and injured turtles: the coastguard, 1530.
  • ☐Green turtle Chelonia mydas — Honest answer: no. It nests almost entirely in the eastern basin and forages in the Levantine and Aegean; in the southern Adriatic it is a genuine rarity. Log any turtle you see as a loggerhead unless you get a clear scute count — green has four pairs of costals and a single pair of prefrontals, with a small rounded head and a smoother shell.

Cetaceans

  • ☐Bottlenose dolphin ★ Tursiops truncatus — Your one genuine chance. Robust, with a short thick beak creased at its base, uniform mid-grey with a subtle paler flank blaze, and a tall falcate dorsal. Coastal, in groups of three to fifteen, resident along both Puglian coasts, and frequently associating with trawlers and mussel farms. Watch for slow rolling group surfacing sequences, occasional loud tail slaps, and deliberate bow-riding near boats.
  • ☐Striped dolphin Stenella coeruleoalba — A dark stripe from eye to anus plus a pale "shoulder blaze" sweeping up toward the dorsal. The most abundant Mediterranean dolphin, but offshore and deep-water: plausible in the Otranto Channel or the Gulf of Taranto from a boat, not inshore from a kayak.
  • ☐Risso's dolphin Grampus griseus — Blunt, bulbous, beakless head, tall dorsal, and adults extensively scarred to pale grey or near-white by other dolphins' teeth and by squid. A slope and canyon specialist; the Gulf of Taranto slope is a known hotspot. Not from a kayak.
  • ☐Common dolphin Delphinus delphis — The diagnostic hourglass flank pattern — ochre front panel, pale grey rear panel, meeting under the dorsal — and a long slim beak. Once abundant, now scarce in the Mediterranean and effectively absent from the Adriatic. Honest answer: no.
  • ☐Sperm whale ★ Physeter macrocephalus — Boat only, but genuinely there. The Gulf of Taranto holds a regularly observed presence including social units with calves, and is one of the better-documented sperm whale areas in the Ionian, with a long-running photo-identification and acoustic effort based out of Taranto. Diagnostic: a single bushy blow angled forward and to the left, a blocky wrinkled back with a low triangular hump instead of a dorsal fin, and the fluke lifted clear on the terminal dive after a long series of shallow breaths. Half-day trips run out of Taranto; worth checking whether any coincide with your dates.
  • ☐Cuvier's beaked whale ★ Ziphius cavirostris — Near-mythical, and worth knowing is out there. The Gulf of Taranto and the southern Adriatic canyons hold a regular population. A short thick beak with an upcurved mouthline giving a "smiling" profile, a low dorsal set far back, dense linear white scarring on old animals, and old males with a whitish head. Extremely long deep dives, unobtrusive surfacings, and it avoids vessels. Highly vulnerable to naval mid-frequency sonar, with Mediterranean mass strandings on record. Not realistically seeable.

The absence written into the map

  • ☐Mediterranean monk seal ★ ⚑ Monachus monachus — The Mediterranean's only pinniped and one of the world's rarest mammals, with a global population of the order of six to eight hundred animals cored in Greece and Turkey. Italian breeding was extinguished by the mid-twentieth century, the last records in the 1970s. But the coast you are paddling documents its own lost fauna in its place-names: caves called Grotta del Bove Marino — "cave of the sea ox," the Italian vernacular for the monk seal — recur repeatedly along the Otranto–Leuca shore, and you can find them on the chart before you launch. Since roughly the 2010s vagrants have been reappearing in Italian waters including the Adriatic, presumably dispersing from a recovering Greek population, and individuals have been photographed in southern Italian sea caves. The caves here are structurally still suitable: high-tide entrances, internal beaches, darkness. Honest odds in one week: essentially zero — but not structurally impossible, and this is exactly where it would happen. If it happens: do not approach, do not enter the cave, do not post the location, photograph from distance, and report it.

Birds of the Cliff and the Open Sea

Every paddling day · residents and the marine species

These are the birds that are there whatever the date. Two structural facts shape the list. First, from a kayak within a kilometre of the coast, shearwaters and petrels are horizon birds: the single highest-yield seawatching act on the route is standing at Punta Palascìa with binoculars at dawn, not paddling. Second, this is a cliff coast, so it holds a set of species you will not see anywhere else on the trip — and they are all findable from the boat.

Tubenoses — from the headland, not the boat

  • ☐Scopoli's shearwater ★ ⚑ Calonectris diomedea — Large and long-winged, pale sandy-grey-brown above and white below, with a yellow bill with a dark tip and, diagnostically, no clean cap: the brown of the head washes diffusely into the white throat with no sharp border. White "tongues" of the underwing run out into the bases of the primaries. Flight relaxed and unhurried, long lazy glides on bowed, slightly kinked wings, arcing over the swell. It finishes breeding through September and then leaves the Mediterranean for the Atlantic, so a late-September bird is on the departing tail.
  • ☐Yelkouan shearwater ★ ⚑ Puffinus yelkouan — Much smaller, brown and white, with a dark cap extending below the eye, a dark bill, and the key mark: a dirty, dusky vent and undertail. Flight fast, stiff and whirring, with short low glides, hugging the water, usually in flocks and rafts. An Adriatic and Aegean speciality that disperses within the basin rather than leaving it, so it becomes relatively more likely as autumn advances — and your dates sit exactly on the crossover, with Scopoli's the better bet on Day 1 and Yelkouan the better bet by Day 7. The Italian Adriatic colonies of both species are on the Tremiti islands, off the Gargano — not the Salento.
  • ☐Mediterranean storm petrel ★ ⚑ Hydrobates pelagicus melitensis — Tiny and sooty-black with a square white rump patch and, separating the Mediterranean form from the Atlantic one, a prominent white bar on the underwing. Fluttering, bat-like, feet pattering the surface. Offshore only — a ferry-crossing bird.

Cormorants

  • ☐European shag ★ ⚑ Gulosus aristotelis desmarestii — Smaller and slimmer than a cormorant, all dark with an oily bottle-green gloss, a steep forehead and a thin hooked bill. The Mediterranean race has a yellow gape and pale chin, a browner tone and a longer thinner neck — and crucially its juveniles are distinctly white-bellied, so a "cormorant" with a white belly on a Salento rock is a young desmarestii shag. That one line does most of the work. Swims very low, and jumps clear of the water in a forward arc before diving where a great cormorant simply slides under. Strictly marine and rock-loving, present year-round, and genuinely gettable from a kayak on the Otranto–Leuca cliffs.
  • ☐Great cormorant Phalacrocorax carbo sinensis — Bigger, heavier and thick-billed, with yellow-orange bare skin at the bill base. Perches with wings held wide to dry. The phenology is the point: it is largely a winter visitor to southern Italy and the first flocks arrive from late September into October — which is precisely your week, so the cormorants appearing at Alimini and Le Cesine are freshly arrived winterers.

Gulls

  • ☐Yellow-legged gull Larus michahellis — The default gull of the route. Large, mid-grey mantle, bright yellow legs, red orbital ring, heavy bill with a big red gonys spot, fierce pale eye. Harbours, cliffs, ploughed fields, and following your kayak. Juveniles are heavily mottled brown with a black bill and a dark tail band, and are the source of most gull confusion.
  • ☐Audouin's gull ★ ⚑ Ichthyaetus audouinii — The gull prize, and unlikely, but check every candidate. Pale grey above with a white head, a coral-red bill with a black band near the tip and a yellow tip, dark olive-grey to blackish legs, a dark eye, and a gently sloping forehead giving a curiously gentle expression. The behaviour is diagnostic: it hunts live fish by dipping and flying low over the water like a big tern, and rarely scavenges — an elegant gull that will not be at the bar. A Mediterranean endemic; Italian breeding is mainly Sardinia, Sicily and the smaller islands, and in Puglia it is a scarce visitor.
  • ☐Mediterranean gull ⚑ Ichthyaetus melanocephalus — Realistic, and worth the comparison. Autumn adults have pure white primaries — no black in the wingtip at all, a heavier slightly drooping red-and-black bill, longer legs, and a dark bandit-mask smudge behind the eye. First-winters are strongly contrasted, with a dark mask, dark wing coverts and a black tail band. Nests colonially in the Adriatic, including the salt pans of northern Puglia, and disperses along both coasts by autumn.
  • ☐Black-headed gull Chroicocephalus ridibundus — Common on your dates and misleadingly named in autumn: a white head with a neat dark ear-smudge, dark red bill and legs, and the diagnostic clean white leading edge to the outer wing with a black trailing tip. Arrives in numbers from late summer, so by now flocks are on harbours, salt pans and ploughed land.
  • ☐Slender-billed gull Chroicocephalus genei — Possible at the salt pans. White head all year, and a long sloping forehead running into a long pale bill with no obvious gonys angle, long neck and pale eye, giving a strangely stretched look.

Terns

  • ☐Sandwich tern ★ Thalasseus sandvicensis — The tern you will actually see. Large and pale grey above, with a long black bill tipped bright yellow — that is the mark — a shaggy black nape crest, and black legs. Patrols offshore, hovers, then plunge-dives hard; a grating "kirr-ick" call. Along both coasts through autumn.
  • ☐Common tern Sterna hirundo — Migrating in September but thinning. Red bill, black-tipped and duller in autumn, red-orange legs, and translucent inner primaries seen against the light.
  • ☐Black tern Chlidonias niger — A genuine late-September passage possibility over Alimini and Le Cesine. Autumn birds pale grey above and white below with a dark cap and a dark shoulder smudge at the breast side, and they dip to the surface rather than plunging.
  • ☐Little tern Sternula albifrons — Honest entry: you have missed it. It breeds on Ionian and Adriatic beaches and salt pans, it is tiny with a uniquely fast flickering wingbeat and a yellow black-tipped bill, and it is an early migrant — the great majority have left for Africa by late September.

The cliff birds

  • ☐Blue rock thrush ★ Monticola solitarius — Very achievable and worth the look. The male is uniform slate-blue, appearing black at distance, slim with a long straight bill; the female dark brown with fine pale scaling below. The behaviour is diagnostic: sits bolt upright on the highest point of a cliff, a tower or a ruin, flicks its wings, dips its tail, then drops vertically out of sight. Silent at this season. Look on the Otranto–Leuca cliffs, the coastal towers, and the walls at Egnazia.
  • ☐Crag martin Ptyonoprogne rupestris — Stocky and plain brown, pale-throated, with dark undertail coverts contrasting against the pale breast and the clincher, a row of small white spots across the spread tail, visible whenever it fans. Flies slowly and deliberately, hugging the rock face and banking hard against it. A short-distance migrant, so present now and often the last martin-like bird still around in November.
  • ☐Pallid swift ★ Apus pallidus — The most useful non-obvious call of the week. Browner and paler than common swift, with a larger, more diffuse pale throat patch, blunter wing-tips, a subtly slower looser wingbeat, a scaly pale-fringed look to the underparts and a darker mask against the pale throat. But the decisive argument is phenology: pallid swift is double-brooded and late, still feeding young into September and lingering in southern Italy into October, whereas common swift has largely left Italy by mid-August. So a swift screaming round the old town of Otranto, Gallipoli or Lecce in late September is almost certainly this, and Salento is a stronghold with colonies in the walls and cornices.
  • ☐Alpine swift Tachymarptis melba — Possible on passage, not to be promised. Much larger and browner, with a white belly and white throat separated by a brown breast-band, and long scythe wings with deep slow beats.
  • ☐Peregrine ★ Falco peregrinus — Resident on these sea cliffs. Broad-based, sharply pointed wings, a short tail and a heavy chest — the "anchor" silhouette — with a dark hood and a thick blunt moustachial wedge and barred underparts.
  • ☐Common kestrel Falco tinnunculus — It hovers, and on this route nothing else does, which makes the entry easy. The male has a blue-grey head and tail with a broad black terminal band and a rufous spotted back.
  • ☐Lesser kestrel ★ Falco naumanni — The honest disappointment, and worth knowing why. Smaller and daintier, the male with an unspotted brick-red mantle, a plain blue-grey head with no moustachial stripe, a blue-grey panel in the greater coverts, and pale claws where kestrel's are black. Colonial, noisy and insectivorous, hawking rather than hovering. Puglia holds the largest Italian population, thousands of pairs nesting under the roof tiles of the historic centres of the Alta Murgia towns — Gravina, Altamura, Santeramo — and it is one of the great wildlife spectacles of southern Italy. But it is a trans-Saharan migrant that gathers in pre-migratory roosts in late August and has largely departed by mid-September. You have missed it by a fortnight, and the Murgia is inland from your route anyway. File under: reasons to come back in June.
  • ☐Rock dove Columba livia — Genuinely wild-type birds use the Salento sea caves, and you will flush them by paddling in. Wild-type marks: two clean narrow black wingbars on a plain pale-grey mantle, a white rump, and a clean white underwing. Any chequering, mottling or odd colour means feral.
  • ☐Jackdaw Corvus monedula — Common at ruins and masserie. A small compact crow with a pale grey shawl over the nape and a startling pale grey-white eye; clipped "kya" calls in tight aerobatic flocks. Nests in cliff holes, town walls and archaeological masonry. Egnazia is a certainty.
  • ☐Raven Corvus corax — Massive, with a deep wedge-shaped tail, shaggy throat hackles, a heavy bill and a resonant "kronk"; soars and barrel-rolls. In Puglia largely a Murgia and Gargano bird. Unlikely on the flat Salento coast; look for it over the Valle d'Itria and the Murgia escarpment.

Birds on Passage

Late September into the first days of October

This is the section that makes your dates worth having, and the section that requires the most honesty, because migration is a moving target and half of what a regional bird list contains has already gone south. What follows is triaged: the ones genuinely moving through Salento in the third and fourth weeks of September are marked as such, and the ones you have missed are here so that you stop looking for them.

Is the Strait of Otranto really a bottleneck?

Partly, and less than the hype. It is the shortest sea crossing between Italy and the Balkans, about seventy-two kilometres, so it genuinely concentrates migrants — but it matters more in spring, when birds arriving from Greece and Albania make landfall in Salento, than in autumn, when Salento functions as a coastal funnel and a departure point for birds heading out over the Ionian. Italy's famous raptor bottlenecks are elsewhere, the Strait of Messina above all. So: real, but do not expect a spectacle.

Whatever is concentrated will show at dawn on the coast at Punta Palascìa, and over the Alimini basin, and best of all after a night of northerly wind. If you get one clear morning with a tramontana behind it, that is the morning to be on the headland at six.

Raptors — the realistic four

  • ☐Osprey ★ Pandion haliaetus — Prime time: mid-September to October, and a genuinely thrilling and realistic sighting from a kayak or over Alimini. A white head with a broad dark eye-stripe, long arched gull-like wings with black carpal patches, white underbody. Hovers heavily, plunges feet-first, and shakes itself in mid-air after rising from a dive.
  • ☐Marsh harrier ★ Circus aeruginosus — One of your most likely raptors; passage peaks mid-September to early October. Long-winged, held in a shallow V, quartering low and slow over reeds. Females and juveniles dark chocolate with a creamy crown, throat and forewing patch. Watch the reedbeds at Le Cesine and Alimini.
  • ☐Pallid harrier ★ Circus macrourus — A genuine rarity that has become a regularly recorded autumn migrant in Italy, and mid-September to October is exactly the window — one where your dates help. The juvenile is the identification: like Montagu's, but with a pale collar or "boa" set off by dark neck-sides and a dark cheek patch, and a more contrasting face.
  • ☐Red-footed falcon ★ Falco vespertinus — Plausible on your dates; the Adriatic flyway carries flocks through September. A small long-winged falcon hawking insects at dusk over stubble, often in loose groups. The male is slate-grey with rusty-red thighs and vent and red feet and cere; the juvenile has a dark moustache and cap, a barred tail and buff underparts.

Raptors — the ones to stop scanning for

  • ☐Honey buzzard Pernis apivorus — The Italian peak is late August to the first ten days of September, so by now the flow is essentially over and you get stragglers, mostly juveniles. Marks anyway: a small protruding head on a long neck, a long tail with two narrow bars near the base and one broad terminal bar, and a flat-winged, oddly pigeon-headed glide.
  • ☐Montagu's harrier Circus pygargus — Peaks late August to mid-September; possible but thinning. The juvenile is the challenge: unstreaked orange-buff underparts, a white rump, and the key mark, dark secondaries seen from below forming a dark trailing panel.
  • ☐Black kite Milvus migrans — Largely gone from Italy by mid-September. Forked tail, twisting in flight, uniformly brown. Unlikely.
  • ☐Eleonora's falcon Falco eleonorae — A late breeder that departs for Madagascar mainly in late October, but its Italian colonies are on Sardinian, Sicilian and Tuscan islets and there are none in Puglia. A genuine rarity in Salento. The dark morph — all sooty, long-winged, hawking insects offshore — is worth knowing in case.
  • ☐Egyptian vulture ★ Neophron percnopterus — A heartbreaking entry. A small vulture, white with black flight feathers, a wedge-shaped white tail, a bare yellow face and a shaggy ruff; juveniles all dark brown. Puglia holds one of Italy's last remnant nuclei — a handful of pairs in the Murgia and Gargano, subject of long-running conservation projects and supplementary feeding. Departure is in September. Theoretically possible as a migrant; realistically no — and the reason is simply that there are almost none left.

Passerines and near-passerines — genuinely present

  • ☐Northern wheatear Oenanthe oenanthe — Common on your dates, on bare ground, tracks and stone walls. White rump and a black inverted T on the tail; autumn birds warm buffy with a pale supercilium and pale wing fringes. Bobs, flicks, runs.
  • ☐Whinchat Saxicola rubetra — A prime late-September migrant, on fences, thistles and wire. The entry is the comparison with the resident stonechat: whinchat has a broad white supercilium, a streaked back, and white patches at the tail base; stonechat has a plain dark head, no supercilium and a plain dark tail.
  • ☐Spotted flycatcher Muscicapa striata — Classic late-September passage. Plain grey-brown with a streaked forecrown and diffusely streaked breast and long wings, and the diagnostic behaviour: sits upright and conspicuous on an open perch, sallies out, and returns to the same perch.
  • ☐Pied flycatcher Ficedula hypoleuca — A very common September migrant in masseria trees and gardens. Autumn birds brown above with a bold white wing panel and white tertial fringes, white below, constantly flicking the wings and cocking the tail.
  • ☐Willow warbler Phylloscopus trochilus — One of the commonest passerine migrants of the period. Long primary projection, pale flesh-coloured legs, yellow-washed juveniles in autumn, a soft rising hueet. Contrast with chiffchaff: dark legs, shorter wings, and numbers building later in October as winterers arrive.
  • ☐European bee-eater ★ Merops apiaster — A major September migrant, and flocks announce themselves before you see them with a liquid rolling prruip from high overhead — learn that one call and you will get them. Most have gone by the end of September, so you are on the tail of it: plausible on Days 1 to 3, unlikely by Day 6.
  • ☐Hoopoe ★ Upupa epops — Present on your dates, and a very likely masseria-track bird. Needs no help: broad black-and-white barred wings flashing in a floppy butterfly flight, a long decurved bill, and the crest fanned on landing.
  • ☐Red-backed shrike Lanius collurio — Passage into mid and late September, mostly juveniles: finely vermiculated crescent barring above and below, and no mask, on a wire or a bramble top.
  • ☐Tawny pipit Anthus campestris — A September migrant on bare stony ground and dunes. Sandy and almost unstreaked on the breast, with a dark row of median coverts standing out on an otherwise plain wing, long legs, a long tail, and a wagtail-like walk.
  • ☐Blackcap Sylvia atricapilla — Numbers surge in late September as migrants pour in, and the reason is botanical: they are here to eat fig, lentisc, elder and bramble. This is the bird that ties the plant chapter to the bird chapter.
  • ☐Black redstart Phoenicurus ochruros — A constantly quivering rust-orange tail on a sooty-grey bird. Numbers build from late September as migrants and winterers arrive, appearing on masseria roofs, trulli and stone walls. A seasonal marker.
  • ☐European robin Erithacus rubecula — Essentially a winter visitor in the far south, which is why the first robin ticking in the masseria garden in late September is a real seasonal event rather than a garden bird.

Passerines you have missed

  • ☐European roller ★ Coracias garrulus — An early migrant, largely gone by early September, which is a real pity: Puglia is a European stronghold for breeding rollers, with active nestbox schemes across the Murgia and Salento. Another argument for June.
  • ☐Woodchat shrike Lanius senator — An early migrant, mostly gone by early September. Unlikely.
  • ☐Black-eared wheatear Oenanthe hispanica / melanoleuca — Mostly gone by mid-September; unlikely but possible. Note the taxonomy has recently been split into western and eastern species, with Italy's southern breeders in the eastern taxon — a split not yet universally adopted, so books disagree.
  • ☐Greater short-toed lark Calandrella brachydactyla — Breeds in Puglian steppe habitat but most have departed. Small and pale, with an unstreaked breast bearing a dark patch at each neck side and a stubby pale conical bill. Possible but thinning.

The Wetlands Behind the Dune

Le Cesine, Alimini, the salt pans — worth a morning off the water

There are four or five wetlands within easy reach of your bases, and one of them — Le Cesine, minutes inland from Torre dell'Orso — is the single highest-value wildlife stop on the route. If a windy morning takes a paddling day away from you, this is where to spend it, and the ask needs to be made the evening before rather than at breakfast, because it involves driving and a decision.

The sites

  • ☐Le Cesine ★ — WWF reserve and Ramsar site, behind the dunes near San Foca. A relict coastal marsh of reedbed, brackish lagoon and pinewood, with hides and marked trails. Late September is excellent: first arriving wintering ducks, marsh harrier over the reeds, waders on the mud (little stint, wood and green and common sandpipers, ruff, black-winged stilt), squacco and purple and night herons, glossy ibis — now regular in Italy and expanding — kingfisher, and enormous hirundine roosts in the reeds at dusk.
  • ☐Laghi Alimini — Two lakes north of Otranto: Alimini Grande, brackish and sea-connected, and Alimini Piccolo, spring-fed and fresher. Reedbeds, superb protected dune juniper woodland, and paddleable water. Herons, grebes, the newly arriving cormorants, and the best raptor-watching airspace on the route.
  • ☐Palude del Capitano ★ — Inside the Porto Selvaggio regional park on the Ionian side, and a geological curiosity as much as a wetland: the spunnulate, karst collapse dolines filled with brackish groundwater and ringed with reed and halophytes. Porto Selvaggio itself is Aleppo pine over limestone cliff with cold freshwater karst springs discharging into the sea — you will feel them from the kayak as a shock of cold water.
  • ☐Torre Guaceto ★ — State nature reserve and marine protected area between Brindisi and Ostuni, directly on the transit north toward Monopoli. Dunes, the Fiume Grande marsh, Posidonia, loggerhead nesting, a turtle rescue facility, and Italy's best-documented marine-reserve fish recovery record — which is why this is the one place on the route where a dusky grouper might swim out to look at you.
  • ☐Salina dei Monaci / Torre Colimena — A former salt pan on the Ionian coast near Manduria, and the easiest flamingos on the route, plus avocet, black-winged stilt and Kentish plover.

The birds that make the detour worth it

  • ☐Greater flamingo ★ Phoenicopterus roseus — Realistic on your dates, and gone in Puglia from scarce visitor to regular and locally breeding: the salt pans at Margherita di Savoia in the north hold hundreds to thousands, with smaller flocks at Salina dei Monaci and along the Alimini coast. Give the behavioural marks rather than the colour: the sharply kinked, downturned pink-and-black bill used upside down, with the head inverted below the surface; the "broken" angled neck at rest; the sudden reveal of crimson-and-black wings on take-off; and the greyest birds in the flock, with dull grey bills and dark legs, are this year's young.
  • ☐Glossy ibis ★ Plegadis falcinellus — Now regular in Italy and expanding, and a genuine bird to get. At distance a black, long-legged, down-curved-billed wader; in decent light, bronze and iridescent green-purple. Flies in lines and V-formations with fast flapping and glides — unlike a heron, which folds its neck.
  • ☐Squacco heron Ardeola ralloides — Small, buff-brown and hunched at the reed edge, and then startlingly white-winged when it flies, which is the entry: a small brown heron that turns into a white bird.
  • ☐Common kingfisher Alcedo atthis — Present at all of these sites. Usually registered as an electric-blue line low over water and a thin high tseee rather than a perched bird.
  • ☐Black-winged stilt Himantopus himantopus — Absurd and unmistakable: black and white, a needle bill, and impossibly long pink legs trailing well beyond the tail in flight.
  • ☐European pond terrapin ★ Emys orbicularis — Not a bird, but this is where it is. Smooth, low, dark olive-black carapace with fine yellow speckling or radiating flecks and a dark head heavily speckled with yellow. Basks on a log or bank and drops in at thirty metres. Compare the invasive pond slider, which is bigger and has a broad red or yellow patch behind the eye and boldly yellow-striped neck — in every ornamental pond and, unfortunately, in wild wetlands too.

Around the Masseria

Walls, cisterns, roofs and the dark — every evening

The richest habitat on this route is a human artefact. A Salento or Valle d'Itria dry-stone wall — a muretto a secco, and the craft of building them is on the UNESCO intangible heritage list — is simultaneously a lizard and gecko basking bank, a snake highway and hibernaculum, a skink refuge, a marten and dormouse run, a little owl perch, and a shaded microhabitat for cyclamen, ferns and navelwort on its north face. Add the cisterns, where the newts live, and the point becomes general: on this route the most biodiverse structures are the ones people built, which is why their abandonment and their bulldozing are both conservation problems.

The operational rule that follows: turn stones, and put them back exactly as they were. The microclimate under a slab takes years to re-form, and it is somebody's entire world.

Lizards and geckos

  • ☐Italian wall lizard ⚑ Podarcis siculus — The default lizard of every wall, dune and ruin here, and an Italian and Adriatic endemic. Bright green over at least the front half of the back, patterned with dark reticulation, whitish-green below, fairly robust with a deep head. The negative marks matter: it lacks the strong sharply defined dark lateral band of the common wall lizard, and lacks blue outer belly-scale spots. Bold, out in the open on hot stone and bare sand, and remarkably tolerant of people — at Egnazia they will run over your feet. Honest note: essentially every wall lizard you see on this route will be this species; the common wall lizard is not to be expected in lowland Salento.
  • ☐Ocellated skink ★ Chalcides ocellatus — A genuine Salento speciality and a good find. Cylindrical, glossy, sausage-shaped, with short weak legs and a smooth almost polished sandy-bronze surface bearing many small black-edged white ocelli in rows. The behaviour is the mark: it does not run like a lizard, it wriggles snake-like into leaf litter, loose sand or a wall crevice — and it is far more often heard as a rustle at the base of a dry-stone wall than seen. Dunes, walls, garden compost, coastal scrub.
  • ☐Moorish gecko Tarentola mauritanica — Large, to about fifteen centimetres, grey-brown, and heavily armoured with big conical keeled tubercles in longitudinal rows, with a broad flat triangular head and broad toe pads bearing claws only on the third and fourth toes. Sits head-down and fully exposed on a vertical wall, often out in daylight or on sun-warmed stone at dusk. Bulky and confident.
  • ☐Turkish gecko Hemidactylus turcicus — Small and delicate, translucent pinkish-cream and often visibly see-through, with fine soft granular tubercles, large dark eyes, and a tail with distinct whorls of spiny tubercles. Strictly nocturnal, keeps to crevices and the wall around a light, and calls with a squeaky chirp — the "mouse squeaking in the masonry" at a masseria is this. Working rule: bulky, warty, bold, grey and out in daylight is Moorish; small, pale, translucent, squeaky and shy is Turkish.

Snakes, and the fact worth having

  • ☐Western whip snake Hierophis viridiflavus — The snake you will actually meet. Slender, up to a metre and a half, with large eyes and a round pupil and a whip-thin tail. South-eastern Italy including Puglia is dominated by the melanistic form: a uniformly glossy blue-black snake. Explosively fast — it crosses a masseria track as a black ribbon — and it will stand its ground, inflate, hiss and strike repeatedly. Harmless, though it can draw blood. It also makes a startling loud rattle through dry vegetation.
  • ☐Four-lined snake ★ Elaphe quatuorlineata — Italy's largest snake, and Puglia is a European stronghold. A metre and a half to two metres, notably heavy-bodied, thick and slow-moving, with a proportionately small head. Adults straw-brown to ochre with four dark longitudinal stripes — only two visible looking down from above — and a dark stripe through the eye. Juveniles look like a different animal: strongly grey with dark saddles and blotches, which is exactly why they get killed as vipers. Famously docile, rarely attempts to bite, climbs into scrub and walls, and raids rodent burrows and birds' nests. The classic masseria "chicken snake," and listed on Annex II of the Habitats Directive.
  • ☐Grass snake Natrix natrix — Dark grey-olive with heavy dark barring on the flanks, a yellow-and-black collar in juveniles, keeled scales and a round pupil. Strongly aquatic, so in Salento it is confined to permanent water — Alimini, Le Cesine, canals and cisterns — and genuinely possible at those sites but not on the open coast. Its more aquatic relative the dice snake, chequered with a more pointed head and upward-and-forward-set eyes, is also local here.
  • ☐Aesculapian snake Zamenis longissimus — Unlikely on this route. Smooth, glossy uniform olive-bronze to nearly two metres with fine white flecking along the scale edges; a superb climber that spirals up trunks and walls. In Puglia it is local and mainly a wooded Gargano and northern animal; in Salento it is scarce to absent. The southern-Italian sister species, pale straw with four thin dark lines, may or may not reach Puglia — the literature is unsettled.
The viper question — and a correction to an appealing myth

You will be told there are no vipers in the Salento. It is very nearly true, and it is not true. Since this is exactly the sort of claim a single record overturns, here is the actual position.

Italy has four viper species, and three of them — the adder, Orsini's viper and the nose-horned viper — occur nowhere near Puglia. So the question is entirely about the asp viper in its southern form, Vipera aspis hugyi. It is present in Puglia on the Gargano and the Murgia. And it is also recorded in the Salento — but as one of the peninsula's rarest reptiles: ecologically selective, tied to genuinely intact habitat, and with a punctiform distribution of a handful of known localities rather than a population.

The awkward part, for a book that would prefer to reassure you, is where those localities are. On the Adriatic side they are given as San Cataldo, Otranto and Santa Cesarea; on the Ionian side, the Arneo and Ugento. Which is to say: your Day 3 lunch stop, the town you walk round that evening, and quite possibly your Day 4 beach. The best-known population, at Frassanito just north of Otranto, appears to be the only one holding decent numbers and has been the subject of a dedicated field study. Local papers treat a sighting as news precisely because it is so uncommon.

So the honest framing is this. The overwhelming majority of snakes you meet on this coast will be the glossy blue-black whip snake or the heavy, docile four-lined snake, and neither is venomous; a snake crossing a track in front of you is still almost certainly good news and worth stopping to look at properly. But almost is doing real work in that sentence, and the conclusion is the one that was always sensible: look before you put a hand into a dry-stone wall, and look before you reach under a rock or into an overhang you cannot see behind. That rule costs nothing and it covers vipers, centipedes and fireworms at once.

On the venom, since the point of knowing is to be calm rather than frightened: an aspis bite is a medical event and not usually a grave one, dangerous chiefly to children, the frail, or anyone who delays treatment. The animal is small, retiring, and would much rather you went away.

Tortoise, and the amphibians that are actually here

  • ☐Hermann's tortoise ★ ⚑ Testudo hermanni — Domed yellow-and-black carapace. The marks that separate it from the spur-thighed tortoise are at the back end: Hermann's has a divided, two-part supracaudal scute and a horny spur at the very tip of the tail, and no enlarged tubercle on the thigh; spur-thighed has a single supracaudal, no tail spur, and a prominent thigh tubercle. Present in Salento macchia, garrigue and abandoned olive groves, though heavily reduced by fire, deep ploughing and collection, and with the local populations taxonomically muddied by decades of released captive stock. Non-obvious and useful: late September and October are among the better times to find one. Tortoises aestivate through the worst summer heat and become active again when the first autumn rain breaks it — so a warm morning after rain on your dates is a genuine chance. Walk slowly and listen for a heavy rustle in dry leaf litter.
  • ☐Italian tree frog ⚑ Hyla intermedia — Three to five centimetres, smooth, bright leaf-green, with a dark stripe from the nostril through the eye along the flank and adhesive toe discs; the separation from the European tree frog is that the flank stripe does not loop strongly upward at the groin. Sits on a bramble or a reed leaf by day. Water-dependent and local here, and mostly silent in late September — with occasional calling after rain.
  • ☐Green toad ⚑ Bufotes balearicus — The better bet, and a dune and masseria-garden animal. Pale grey-cream with bold irregular olive-green marbling and often small red warts, and a long, high, musical trill unlike any other Italian amphibian.
  • ☐Italian newt ★ ⚑ Lissotriton italicus — A lovely Salento speciality and endemic to central-southern Italy. Very small, seven or eight centimetres, olive-brown with a yellow-orange unspotted or lightly spotted belly and throat and a dark stripe through the eye; breeding males have only a low ridge rather than a crest. Look in masseria cisterns, stone water troughs and shaded tanks — exactly the sort of thing worth checking with a torch while everyone else is at dinner.
  • ☐Italian pool frog Pelophylax spp. — The green frogs plopping off every canal bank and cistern edge, with prominent dorsolateral folds, paired lateral vocal sacs in males, and a loud chuckling chorus.
  • ☐Apennine yellow-bellied toad ⚑ Bombina pachypus — Not on this route, and included because it is worth knowing why. It is spectacular — warty grey-brown above, a brilliant yellow-and-black marbled belly, a heart-shaped pupil unique in Europe, and the unkenreflex, arching the back and limbs to flash the warning belly. But the Italian animal is an endemic in steep decline from chytrid fungus, and in Puglia it is essentially confined to the Gargano.

Mammals — mostly signs rather than animals

  • ☐Crested porcupine ★ Hystrix cristata — Unmistakable if seen: a large rodent with a mantle of black-and-white banded quills to thirty centimetres. But the realistic encounter is indirect: shed quills on tracks (pick one up — hollow, light, astonishingly stiff), conical feeding pits dug for roots and bulbs, and cleanly gnawed prickly-pear pads. Strictly nocturnal; when alarmed it rattles its hollow rear quills and reverses into the threat. Native or introduced? Genuinely contested, and the open question is more interesting than a false answer: the majority view is an ancient human-mediated introduction from North Africa, plausibly Roman-era, supported by low genetic differentiation between Italian and North African populations. Italian Pleistocene Hystrix fossils exist, but are attributed to different, extinct species. Fully protected under Italian law regardless, and expanding strongly in Puglia.
  • ☐Stone marten Martes foina — The likely mustelid, and probably what is thumping about above your bedroom at two in the morning. A creamy-white throat bib, usually divided or forked and extending onto the insides of the forelegs, and a pinkish nose; long-bodied, bushy-tailed, grey-brown. The pine marten has an undivided yellowish-buff bib and a dark nose, and is a woodland animal effectively absent from Salento. Lives in masseria roofs, trulli and dry-stone walls.
  • ☐Garden dormouse ★ Eliomys quercinus — Charismatic and noisy. A bold black bandit mask through and behind the eye, very large mobile ears, grey-brown above and clean white below, and the clincher, a long tail ending in a flattened black-and-white brush. Lives in walls, outbuildings and orchards, and is aggressive and vocal — a sudden chattering shriek from a fig tree at night is very likely this.
  • ☐Red fox Vulpes vulpes — Southern Italian foxes are often lean and pale-flanked. The signs: scats deposited conspicuously in the middle of tracks and on rocks, full of fig seeds and beetle wing-cases in autumn, and a narrow almost single-file trail line. From a kayak at dusk you may well see one trotting a beach and working the strandline.
  • ☐Least weasel Mustela nivalis — Tiny, barely twenty centimetres plus tail. Rich chocolate-brown above with a sharply defined pure white underside whose border is irregular and wavy, and a short brown tail with no black tip. Italian weasels do not turn white in winter. Registered as a low bounding brown flicker across a track, then gone.
  • ☐Italian hare ★ ⚑ Lepus corsicanus — A genuine prize hiding in plain sight. The European hare is widespread here and has been massively released for hunting, but the native central-southern species is a separate animal, and Puglia is one of its better remaining regions. Marks: a brighter rusty-red nape and shoulder, a more sharply contrasting black-above-white-below tail, a whiter belly, and a shorter more rounded head. So: the hare standing in the stubble at dawn may be a species most Italians have never heard of — look at the shoulder colour. Some authors think the two are safely separable only in the hand, which is worth knowing before you claim one.
  • ☐Wild boar Sus scrofa — Signs rather than animals: extensive rootings turning over track edges and vineyard rows, muddy wallows, mud-rubbed trunks and posts, and a print showing two large central toes plus two rear dewclaw impressions in soft ground. Historically scarce or absent in Salento, and since the 2000s explosively expanded — so this is a novel arrival in a landscape with no history of it, and a serious problem for vineyards and road safety. Italy has legislated repeatedly for population reduction, sharpened by African swine fever containment, and the resulting restriction zones can affect access to woodland; worth asking about locally if you plan to walk.
  • ☐European hedgehog Erinaceus europaeus — No identification problem in the south, since the white-breasted species reaches only the far north-east. Realistically registered as a road casualty and as a snuffling, surprisingly noisy presence in a masseria garden at night.

Bats

  • ☐Schreiber's bent-winged bat ★ Miniopterus schreibersii — The classic cave bat of southern Italy and the one most often named for Apulian caves. Medium-sized, with a strikingly short, high, domed forehead and very small triangular ears, and — hence the name — an extremely long third finger whose terminal phalanx folds sharply back at rest. Strictly cave-dwelling all year, in huge dense clusters on cave ceilings.
  • ☐Greater horseshoe bat Rhinolophus ferrumequinum — The largest European horseshoe bat, and the posture is the entry: it hangs free from the ceiling, fully wrapped in its own wings like a suspended plum, with the horseshoe-shaped noseleaf. The lesser horseshoe does the same at sparrow size.
  • ☐Long-fingered bat ★ Myotis capaccinii — A Puglian speciality: a cave-dwelling bat that trawls the water surface with disproportionately large feet, hunting low over still water.
  • ☐Kuhl's pipistrelle Pipistrellus kuhlii — The ubiquitous Mediterranean building bat, round every masseria light. In a torch beam it has a real field mark: a clean white trailing edge to the wing membrane between the fifth finger and the foot.
  • ☐European free-tailed bat ★ Tadarida teniotis — The best bat entry available, because you can identify it without a detector. Huge — thirty-five to forty centimetres of wingspan — cigar-shaped, with enormous forward-projecting ears and a long tail protruding well beyond the membrane, in fast high straight flight over cliffs and towns. And its call is low enough to be audible to the unaided human ear: a hard metallic tsick… tsick from high overhead at dusk. Stand outside after dinner and listen up.
Cave etiquette, and why the month matters

Late September is the worst possible time to disturb a bat roost. Mediterranean bats are in pre-hibernation hyperphagia and autumn swarming, laying down the fat that determines whether they survive the winter; a flushed cluster burns energy it cannot replace, and disturbance at this stage has measurable survival costs.

So: no torches on ceilings; no shouting or whistling to test the echo, which is a standard sea-cave habit and a genuinely damaging one; no flash photography; stay near the entrance; do not linger; and turn back if you smell ammonia or see a guano floor, which means an occupied roost. Many Salento caves are designated bat roosts within protected sites and entry is restricted or gated. And do not carry unwashed caving gear between countries: white-nose fungus is present in Europe and must not be moved around.

Birds of the courtyard and the olive grove

  • ☐Little owl ★ Athene noctua — The masseria owl, and almost guaranteed. Squat and flat-headed with fierce yellow eyes under white eyebrows, boldly white-spotted brown. Frequently out in daylight on a wall, roof or telegraph pole, bobbing and glaring; a sharp rising kiew and a yelping alarm.
  • ☐Barn owl Tyto alba — Resident in masseria outbuildings, trulli and church towers, and fine on your dates. White heart-shaped facial disc, long-legged, and a silent, moth-like, wavering flight; a harsh drawn-out shriek, not a hoot.
  • ☐Eurasian scops owl Otus scops — The honest miss, and the one everybody expects. Tiny, grey-brown, bark-patterned, with small ear tufts and an upright bark-mimicking posture, and a voice that is unmistakable: a monotonous single kiu repeated every two or three seconds, like a sonar ping. But it is a summer migrant to Italy, calling mainly from March to July, and by late September the great majority have left for Africa and calling has ceased. Some Mediterranean populations are partly resident, so it is not impossible — but do not expect it.
  • ☐Sardinian warbler ⚑ Curruca melanocephala — The default scrub bird, resident, and vocal now. The male has a glossy black hood extending below the eye, a bright red orbital ring, and a white throat cut sharply against grey underparts, with a long dark white-edged tail. The voice is the entry: a hard rattling scold like a pebble shaken in a tin, delivered from inside a lentisc, followed by a brief clumsy dash to the next bush.
  • ☐Cetti's warbler Cettia cetti — Rich rufous-brown, with a short rounded tail frequently cocked and flicked, skulking in dense damp scrub. Voice is the whole entry: an explosive, startlingly loud chip! chipipi-chipipi! delivered year-round including now, from a bird you will see for two seconds at most. Every ditch, canal and reed edge on the route.
  • ☐Italian sparrow ★ ⚑ Passer italiae — A common bird with a remarkable story. It looks like a house sparrow wearing a Spanish sparrow's head: a chestnut crown running right to the forehead with no grey central stripe, clean white cheeks, a black bib, and pale lightly marked flanks. It is a stabilised hybrid species, arising from house sparrow crossed with Spanish sparrow, and one of the best-studied cases of homoploid hybrid speciation in birds. Since Spanish sparrow also occurs in the far south, check flocks: true Spanish sparrow has heavy black streaking on the flanks and blotched black on the breast. Tree sparrow, also present, has a chocolate crown, a black cheek spot and a white collar.
  • ☐Cirl bunting Emberiza cirlus — The male has a black-and-yellow striped face pattern and an olive-grey breast band above a yellow belly, with chestnut flanks. Edges of olive groves and terraced slopes — more a Valle d'Itria bird than a coastal Salento one.
  • ☐European green woodpecker Picus viridis — Green above with a yellow-green rump, a red crown and a black mask; the male has a red centre to the black moustache. A loud yaffling laugh, a deeply undulating flight, and the habit of feeding on ants on open ground. Ignore claims of a distinctive Italian race; the real recent change is that the Iberian bird is now treated as a separate species.
  • ☐Eurasian jay Garrulus glandarius — Pinkish-brown with a brilliant blue-and-black barred wing panel, a white rump against a black tail, and a harsh tearing screech. The late-September behaviour is worth pointing at: busily caching acorns from the holm oaks and the Vallonea oaks — planting next century's woodland while you watch.
  • ☐European serin Serinus serinus — Tiny, with a very stubby bill, streaked yellow-green with a bright yellow rump, and a jangling "broken glass" trill from a wire or treetop.

The False Autumn

Why almost everything in flower is a leafless bulb

This is the most interesting botanical fact of your dates, and it reverses northern expectations completely. Late September in Salento is not autumn. It is the last week of a nine-month drought, and almost the entire flora is dormant, brown, hard and shut down. The consequence is that nearly everything in flower on your dates is a leafless geophyte — flowering on a stored bulb or corm, triggered by heat or day-length or the first rain, and putting up its leaves only afterwards. Two of the most conspicuous shrubs on the cliffs are standing bare because it is dry, not because it is cold, and they are about to come into leaf.

And the whole system is poised on the first serious rain. If it breaks while you are there, the landscape changes visibly within forty-eight hours: a green flush, fungi, amphibians calling out of season, tortoises up and walking, and the first orchid rosettes pushing through. Guidebooks describe a Mediterranean autumn that does not exist. The real event is the drought breaking.

In flower now — the leafless bulbs

  • ☐Sea squill ★ Drimia maritima — The plant of your dates, and conspicuous at two hundred metres across a burnt hillside. A massive bulb, often half-exposed above ground, throwing up a single completely leafless spike a metre to a metre and a half tall — occasionally two — packed with dozens to hundreds of small white stars each with a green midrib, opening from the bottom upward over several weeks. It flowers August into October and is entirely leafless while flowering; the broad glaucous basal rosette appears afterwards, through autumn and winter, and withers by early summer. Three extras: it is highly toxic (cardiac glycosides) and was the classic Mediterranean rat poison; it is grazing-resistant, so abundance indicates heavy grazing and disturbance; and it was traditionally planted to mark field boundaries and graves — so a straight line of squill spikes across a Salento field may literally be a property line.
  • ☐Autumn squill Prospero autumnale — Small, five to twenty centimetres, a slender spike of a few pale lilac-violet stars with no bracts, flowering August to October usually before the thread-like leaves appear. Habitat: short grazed turf, rock crevices and the thin soil of the limestone shelf. Genuinely in flower on your dates — but you have to be looking down, and it is exactly the sort of thing people walk over all week.
  • ☐Autumn crocus Colchicum spp. — Leafless pink-lilac goblets emerging straight out of bare ground. The separation from a true crocus is worth carrying: Colchicum has six stamens and three separate styles, and its ovary is underground; a true crocus has three stamens and a single style branching into three. Extremely toxic. In Puglia look for the small early-autumn species in which the leaves come up with the flowers. Note honestly that the region's true autumn crocuses, including a southern Puglian endemic, flower mainly in October and November, so the very earliest could just overlap your last two or three days — worth looking down at short grazed turf on the Murge.
  • ☐Ivy-leaved cyclamen ★ Cyclamen hederifolium — Pink reflexed flowers with a dark magenta mouth, on twisted stalks, appearing before the leaves; the ivy-shaped silver-marbled leaves follow. Flowers September to November, so late September is the very start of its season — best in shade under holm oak and along old walls, especially in the wooded Valle d'Itria. Include the behaviour, because it is remarkable: after flowering the stalk coils into a tight spring, drawing the developing capsule down to ground level, where ants carry the sticky seeds away. An autumn cyclamen here is this species; the spring one is a different plant.
  • ☐Autumn lady's-tresses ★ Spiranthes spiralis — The one orchid you could plausibly find. Ten to twenty centimetres, with a single spiral rank of small white-and-green flowers and a separate basal leaf rosette off to one side, in short grazed turf, flowering August to October. Recorded in Puglia. Which brings us to the honest paragraph below.
Orchids: you have come at the wrong time, and it is worth saying plainly

Salento and the Murgia are of European importance for orchids, with a spectacular Ophrys flora including several narrow Apulian endemics, plus Serapias, Anacamptis and Himantoglossum. It is one of the genuine botanical reasons to come here.

But the entire season runs February to May, with a few into early June. In late September you will see nothing in flower — at best the freshly emerging basal leaf rosettes of Ophrys and Serapias, which push up with the autumn rains from October onward. If orchids matter to you, the month is April. The single exception is autumn lady's-tresses, above.

Fruiting — which is what late September is really about

  • ☐Mastic Pistacia lentiscus — The structural shrub of the macchia, and the diagnostic pair of marks is on the leaf: pinnate with an EVEN number of leaflets and no terminal leaflet, on a conspicuously winged, grooved rachis. Strongly resinous when crushed. Its relative terebinth has a terminal leaflet, an unwinged rachis, and is deciduous. Fruit ripens red then black exactly now, and the black-fruit stage is the one you will see, being stripped by blackcaps, robins, Sardinian warblers and blackbirds. The shrub-and-warbler mutualism is the entry: the migrant warblers in the passage chapter are here largely because the lentisc is fruiting.
  • ☐Carob ★ Ceratonia siliqua — Dark glossy pinnate leaves and magnificently gnarled trunks in old Salento groves, with long flattened leathery brown pods to twenty centimetres ripening and hanging now — you can pick fallen pods up. Two things to know: the seeds are famously uniform in weight and are the origin of the carat; and the tree has an odd flowering habit, with small red petal-less flowers borne directly on old wood in autumn, with a strong and frankly unpleasant smell — so you may find the same tree flowering and fruiting simultaneously, which is genuinely arresting.
  • ☐Prickly pear ⚠ Opuntia ficus-indica — Introduced and thoroughly naturalised, and late September is peak fichi d'India, in yellow, orange and blood-red forms. A charming local detail: the first summer flush is often deliberately removed to force a second, larger, later crop ripening into autumn. The hazard is not the big spines but the glochids — near-invisible hair-fine barbs that will ruin an afternoon. And look for pads coated in white waxy fluff: the cochineal scale insect has spread destructively through Italian prickly-pear stands in recent years, killing whole plantations.
  • ☐Myrtle Myrtus communis — Opposite, small, glossy, pointed aromatic leaves which, held up to the light, show translucent oil dots — the best single mark. The blue-black berries ripen October to December, so on your dates expect them green or just turning.
  • ☐Holm oak Quercus ilex — Dark evergreen, leaves grey-felted beneath, with margins that are spiny on low and young growth and entire higher up — the variability is itself the mark. Acorns ripen and drop September to November, so acorn fall is under way and the jays are caching.
  • ☐Macedonian oak — the fragno ★ ⚑ Quercus trojana — A first-rate and little-known fact for the Valle d'Itria stay. This deciduous oak is the signature tree of the Murge and the Valle d'Itria, and its natural western limit in the world lies in Puglia — the fragno woods around Ceglie Messapica and Martina Franca are the westernmost stands anywhere. Its wood is what the Martina Franca capocollo is smoked over.
  • ☐Vallonea oak ★ ⚑ Quercus ithaburensis subsp. macrolepis — You are having dinner at Tricase on Day 2, and the monumental tree is a few minutes outside the town: called the Quercia dei Cento Cavalieri — the Oak of the Hundred Knights, for a legend that it sheltered a hundred mounted men — has a crown spread usually quoted around seven hundred square metres and an age quoted at seven to nine hundred years. Both figures are traditional rather than measured. Botanically the diagnostic feature is the acorn cup: enormous, woody, and covered in long recurved reflexed scales like a shaggy crown or a sea urchin, utterly unlike any other European oak. That cup is the vallonea of commerce, exported in bulk for tanning leather because of its very high tannin content. Acorns take two years to mature and ripen September to October, so your dates are exactly right to pick a cup up off the ground. And the real story is the phytogeography: this is an eastern-Mediterranean species whose only native Italian stands are in Salento — a relict of a Greek and Anatolian flora stranded on the heel of Italy.

The macchia, the garrigue, and the three plants that make the point

  • ☐Sticky fleabane Dittrichia viscosa — The single most conspicuous flower of your dates, and almost always omitted from guidebooks. A sticky, intensely aromatic yellow-daisy subshrub covering every roadside, field margin, abandoned lot and disturbed slope from September to November, and a major late-season nectar source for the last butterflies, bees and hoverflies. If one plant defines late-September Salento visually, this is it.
  • ☐Tree spurge ★ Euphorbia dendroides — An inverted seasonality worth pointing out explicitly. A dome of bare grey-white forking stems through late summer, because it is deciduous in response to drought, not cold, and it re-greens with the autumn rains. The dead-looking bushes on the cliffs are the ones about to come into leaf.
  • ☐Thorny burnet ★ ⚑ Sarcopoterium spinosum — A phytogeographical prize and the right note to end the section on. A dense, perfectly hemispherical, intricately spiny dwarf shrub thirty to sixty centimetres high, with tiny pinnate leaves, zig-zag spine-tipped twigs, and fleshy red fruiting heads after flowering. Like the tree spurge it is summer-deciduous, leafing out with the autumn rains. It is an eastern-Mediterranean species, and in Italy it is restricted to a handful of Puglian localities plus a couple of other southern coastal sites — so its Salento garrigue is effectively a stranded fragment of Greek and Levantine landscape, matching the Vallonea oak exactly. It indicates fire, grazing and thin degraded karst soil.
  • ☐Autumn heather Erica manipuliflora — Worth an entry precisely because it is the exception: one of the very few macchia shrubs genuinely in flower on your dates, with pink bells from late summer through autumn.
  • ☐Rock samphire Crithmum maritimum — It grows exactly where you land a kayak. Succulent, fleshy, blue-green divided leaves on a low sprawling plant, with flat-topped greenish-yellow umbels and later ridged corky fruits, and strongly, pungently resinous when crushed. Flowers roughly July to September, so on your dates you get flowers and fruit on the same plant. It marks the aerohaline zone, immediately above the Lithophyllum trottoir — which means you can photograph the entire vertical zonation of the shore in one frame from the boat. Traditionally pickled and eaten.
  • ☐Mediterranean thyme Thymbra capitata — Hard aromatic cushions on the limestone shelf. This is the smell of the hot Salento rock, and worth crushing a sprig for deliberately so you can attach the name to it.
  • ☐Large-fruited juniper ★ Juniperus macrocarpa — The dune juniper, prostrate and wind-sculpted, with glaucous berries twelve to fifteen millimetres across — much larger than the Phoenician juniper of the cliffs. The dune juniper woodland at Alimini and Punta Prosciutto is outstanding and protected.
  • ☐Sea daffodil ★ Pancratium maritimum — A star when in flower, and flowering is essentially over by late September, so promise the seeds rather than the flowers. Huge white trumpet flowers with spidery tepals, night-scented and hawkmoth-pollinated, growing directly on bare dune sand from July to early September. What you will find now: swollen three-angled green capsules, and then the angular corky jet-black seeds, which float and are dispersed by the sea.
  • ☐Chaste tree Vitex agnus-castus — A shrub of seasonal watercourses, damp dune-backs and dry stream mouths, with palmately compound leaves of five to seven narrow leaflets, grey-downy beneath, strongly aromatic, and long terminal spikes of small lilac-blue flowers. Flowers July to September, so expect late spikes plus the ripening peppercorn-like fruits, historically used as a spice and as an anaphrodisiac — hence the name. Look in the Alimini area.
  • ☐Sea lavenders ⚑ Limonium spp. — Papery pink-violet sprays on the rock shelf, flowering into autumn, growing in the salt-spray zone with rock samphire. Several narrow Salento and Apulian endemics are in this genus, which makes an unremarkable-looking plant one of the region's real botanical distinctions.
  • ☐Caper Capparis spinosa — Cascading out of the masonry at Egnazia and Otranto. Flowers June to August, so on your dates expect the fruits — the cucunci, which are pickled and sold separately from the buds.
  • ☐Aleppo pine Pinus halepensis — Pale grey bark, long slender bright-green paired needles, and small asymmetric shiny reddish cones that persist on the branch for years, in an open irregular crown leaning downwind. Porto Selvaggio's cliffside pinewood is the classic, though most Salento pinete are twentieth-century plantings and are suffering drought- and beetle-driven dieback. Look for the white silken bags of the pine processionary moth in the crowns — whose caterpillars carry seriously urticating hairs, though the famous nose-to-tail processions are a late-winter event, so in September you will see only small young nests.

Small Life

Insects, spiders, and the animal that probably caused tarantism

On these dates the visible life of the countryside is overwhelmingly invertebrate, and a good deal of it is spectacular. Late September is also the acoustic changing of the guard: the cicadas fall silent and the bush-crickets and tree crickets take over the night, the scops owl has gone and left the little owl in possession, the swifts overhead have changed species, and the first robins and black redstarts arrive. If you note nothing else each evening — which owl, which insect, which chat — you will have recorded the turn of the Mediterranean year more precisely than any calendar does.

  • ☐Two-tailed pasha ★ Charaxes jasius — The largest European butterfly, and its second brood flies August to October, so this is its month. Orange-banded on a dark ground, with two tails on each hindwing. Powerfully fast and aggressively territorial; the larva feeds on strawberry tree, and the adults come to fallen figs and to dung — so look at rotting fruit rather than at flowers.
  • ☐Hummingbird hawkmoth Macroglossum stellatarum — Day-flying, hovering at flowers with an audible hum and an extended proboscis, with orange hindwings visible in the blur. Feeding at Dittrichia in numbers on your dates.
  • ☐Oleander hawkmoth ★ Daphnis nerii — A late-summer migrant, and the reason to look at every oleander hedge: the caterpillar is spectacular — large, green, with a blue-and-white eyespot behind the head and a small yellow tail horn. The adult is a beautiful marbled grey-green.
  • ☐Painted lady Vanessa cardui — In autumn migration on your dates, and one of the great insect migrants: some individuals of this species cross the Sahara. Orange-brown with black and white wing-tips.
  • ☐Long-tailed blue Lampides boeticus — Small, with a fine thread-like tail on each hindwing and two black-and-orange eyespots beside it — a false head, which the butterfly presents to predators while facing the other way.
  • ☐Scarce swallowtail Iphiclides podalirius — Pale cream with narrow black tiger-stripes and long tails, gliding more than flapping. Its relative the common swallowtail is yellower with broader black bands.
  • ☐Geranium bronze Cacyreus marshalli — An invasive South African species now on every hotel pelargonium in the Mediterranean. Small, dull brown above with a fine white fringe and a short tail. Worth knowing because it is an easy, guaranteed tick and a genuine biogeographical event.
  • ☐Praying mantis Mantis religiosa — Green or straw-brown, with a dark ringed spot on the inside of each foreleg base. Adults are at their largest and most conspicuous in late summer and autumn, which is now. And look for the extraordinary Empusa pennata, a mantis with a cone-shaped horn on its head and feathered antennae in the male.
  • ☐Egyptian grasshopper Anacridium aegyptium — Large, grey-brown and heavy, and the mark is unmistakable once you look: vertically striped eyes. Common on shrubs around masserie.
  • ☐Small scorpions Euscorpius spp. — Under almost any lifted slab: small, dark, flattened, and harmless — the sting is comparable to a bee's and these are not dangerous animals. Put the stone back exactly as you found it.
The spider that probably caused tarantism — a case of mistaken identity

Salento's pizzica tarantata and the tarantism cult of Galatina are usually presented as pure folklore, but there is a zoological argument underneath and it is a good one.

The ritual was traditionally blamed on the tarantula wolf spider, Lycosa tarantula — a large, ground-dwelling, burrowing wolf spider of dry Mediterranean grassland whose bite is in fact trivial. The far more plausible culprit is the Mediterranean black widow, Latrodectus tredecimguttatus: a black spider marked with thirteen red spots on the abdomen, living in dry grassland and cereal stubble — exactly where harvest labourers worked — and possessing a genuinely neurotoxic venom capable of producing the sweating, cramps, agitation and prolonged distress described in the historical accounts.

So this is a who's who entry in the strict sense: a famous piece of Salento culture, a misidentification that lasted centuries, and two spiders you can actually go and look for in the stubble. Do not handle the second one.

The Ones You'll Stop Noticing

The furniture — learn these first

Every list in this book so far has been built around prizes, endemics and things that are hard to see, which is precisely the wrong filter for a first week in a new place. What you will actually ask about is the bird on every roadside, the crab on every rock, the gull following the boat, and the thing that keeps shouting at you from the ditch. Learn this chapter on day one and the rest of the book becomes usable.

The ones on every track and wire

  • ☐Crested lark Galerida cristata — The archetypal Puglian roadside bird. A spiky pointed crest raised and lowered, sandy streaked plumage, a longish slightly decurved bill, buff rather than white outer tail feathers, and broad wings with rusty underwings. Walks on tracks and car parks, extraordinarily tame, with a melancholy whistled call.
  • ☐Corn bunting Emberiza calandra — Diagnosed by the absence of marks plus the shape: dumpy, plain streaked brown, thick pale bill, no wingbars, no tail flashes, nothing. Sits on a wire; in display flies with its legs dangling; song a jangling accelerando ending in shattered glass.
  • ☐Zitting cisticola Cisticola juncidis — Resident, ubiquitous and always omitted from guidebooks. Tiny and streaked, with a short rounded tail broadly tipped white and constantly fanned, and a monotonous zit… zit… zit delivered in a bouncing song-flight over stubble. You will hear it every day of the trip.
  • ☐Goldfinch Carduelis carduelis — Red face mask, broad gold wingbar, tinkling flight call. Late-September flocks on thistles and teasels are one of the defining sights of the Puglian stubble.
  • ☐Greenfinch Chloris chloris — Chunky, with a thick pale bill, olive-green, with yellow flashes in the wing and at the tail sides, and a wheezy drawn-out dzweee.
  • ☐Hooded crow Corvus cornix — Grey body with a black hood, wings and tail — unmistakable, and on every beach working the strandline and behind every plough.
  • ☐Magpie Pica pica — Unmistakable, but two things people miss: the green-purple iridescence on the long tail and the white windows in the primaries in flight.
  • ☐Common starling Sturnus vulgaris — Autumn flocks building over the olive groves, and by October they will be the dominant bird of the landscape. Winter adults heavily spangled with pale spots and a dark bill.
  • ☐Collared dove Streptopelia decaocto — Pale sandy-grey with a narrow black half-collar and a monotonous three-note coo from every town aerial and masseria roof.
  • ☐White wagtail Motacilla alba — Arriving in numbers from now, and one of the standard strandline birds — a black, white and grey bird that runs, then stops, and pumps its tail continuously.

The ones on every rock and in every pool

  • ☐Marbled rock crab — Already described, and worth repeating here because it is the single most abundant visible animal of the week. Squarish, marbled, in the splash zone, gone sideways into a crack before you finish looking at it.
  • ☐Rayed Mediterranean limpet Patella caerulea — The limpet on every metre of rock: low, ribbed, with an iridescent blue-grey interior. Each one occupies a "home scar" it grinds into the rock and returns to. Note also that the giant ribbed limpet Patella ferruginea is one of the Mediterranean's most endangered marine invertebrates — larger, with strong raised ribs and a scalloped margin — and any record of it matters.
  • ☐Turban topshell Phorcus turbinatus — The small conical grey-purple snail covering the splash zone in thousands, and the shell that most of the hermit crabs are living in.
  • ☐Beadlet anemone Actinia equina — In pools and crevices: a smooth red-brown column with short tentacles and, at the base of the tentacles, a ring of bright blue beads.
  • ☐Blennies Parablennius spp. — The small fish in every shallow pool and crevice: elongate, big-headed, with bushy tentacles above the eyes, propped on their pectoral fins on the bottom, darting from hole to hole. The peacock blenny, with a crest on the male's head, is the showy one.
The strandline is the best notebook on the route

Two hundred metres of Salento strandline in late September will typically give you, in one walk: Posidonia aegagropiles and banquette litter; cuttlebones; the corky black floating seeds and papery capsules of the sea daffodil; the dried crackling "cellophane" sails of Velella; the buoyant green "sea olives" of Posidonia after a flowering year; mermaid's purses — the egg cases of catsharks and skates, which are your direct evidence that there are still sharks and rays offshore; and the empty spires of the two murex snails, Bolinus brandaris and Hexaplex trunculus, from which Tyrian purple was extracted.

That last one is the best archaeology-meets-biology join on the trip. At Egnazia you can paddle over the submerged Roman port and then pick up, off the beach, the shell of the animal that made the empire's purple.

And the running tally worth keeping: the warm-water arrivals

Salento is a front line of Mediterranean warming, and a naturalist can keep a list of species that would have been remarkable thirty years ago and are now ordinary. Ornate wrasse in abundance. The fireworm. The invasive alga Caulerpa cylindracea and the Red Sea seagrass Halophila stipulacea. The blue crab, now a major and bitterly contested invasion in Italian lagoons and coastal fisheries. And the Lessepsian migrants moving west out of the Suez route, including the silver-cheeked toadfish — a pufferfish, seriously toxic, must not be eaten, and whose records are actively wanted — the bluespotted cornetfish, and rabbitfish.

Any of these should be photographed and reported to the Italian citizen-science recording schemes, which turns a paddling holiday into data. That is not a small thing: for several of these species, tourists with cameras are how the range front is actually mapped.

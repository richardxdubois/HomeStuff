How to Use This

And what has deliberately been left out

This follows the blue watch-list boxes in the field companion, and it is deliberately short. You have been birding and looking at wildlife for decades in a continent whose garden birds you already know, so there is no entry here for magpie, goldfinch, hooded crow or red fox. What is in it is the material a European naturalist genuinely has not met: the Mediterranean marine life, which is a different fauna from anything in the Atlantic or the tropics; the endemics and the relicts, several of which exist nowhere else; the hazards; and the small number of honest absences worth knowing about in advance.

Entries give two or three diagnostic marks plus a posture or behaviour, because that is how identification actually works. Every name links to an iNaturalist search, which covers fish, plants and invertebrates as well as birds. ★ marks a genuine prize; ⚑ an endemic or relict; ⚠ the few things that can hurt you. The boxes are for ticking.

The one piece of equipment advice

On this trip the mask and snorkel are the binoculars. Most of what is in this book lives in the top three metres of water, in fifteen to thirty metres of horizontal visibility, and is completely invisible from above the surface. A cheap mask you can put your face into over the side converts an empty blue plane into a populated landscape. Tofino's packing list has mask and snorkel under "optional"; treat it as essential. Bring binoculars too — the seabirds are horizon birds — but if you bring one optical instrument, bring the mask.

One entry was wrong, and it is worth knowing which

The first draft of this volume told you there are no vipers in the Salento — the standard local claim, and the kind of thing that sounds too good to check. It is not true, and the recorded localities are on your route. The corrected entry is in On Land, and it is the reason the rest of this book states its sources of doubt rather than rounding them into confidence.

The Meadow and the Shallows

Days 1, 4 and 6 · put your face in the water

The turn of September into October is the best snorkelling week of the Salento year: the sea is at its annual peak temperature, the water is settled, the warm-water species are at maximum abundance, and the summer's juveniles are still in the shallows. Everything below is realistically within five metres of the surface, and most of it within two.

Seagrass, and how to read a meadow from the surface

  • ☐Neptune grass ⚑ Posidonia oceanica — A flowering plant, not an alga, endemic to the Mediterranean. Ribbon leaves about a centimetre wide with a blunt tip, in bundles of five to eight from a woody rhizome; the clincher is leaf width plus the brown fibrous sheath on the rhizome. It extends one to six centimetres a year, among the slowest-growing plants known, and single clones spread over kilometres.
  • ☐Little Neptune grass Cymodocea nodosa — The look-alike: leaves only two to four millimetres wide, two to four per shoot, finely serrated tips, thin rhizome with no brown sheath. Shallower, more sheltered, sandier ground; pale, sparse, low stands.
  • ☐Banquettes and aegagropiles — Strandline objects, not species, and both worth recognising. Banquettes are the terraced brown berms of shed Posidonia leaves, sometimes over a metre high, layered like puff pastry and solid to walk on: they absorb winter wave energy and hold a large fraction of the beach's sand inside the litter, so mechanically clearing them accelerates the erosion the clearing was meant to prevent. A beach with an intact banquette is a well-managed beach. Aegagropiles are the felted grey-brown "sea balls" of lignified rhizome fibre, rolled by wave action in sandy hollows — the same fibre was harvested historically as mattress stuffing.
The four signatures, from the boat

Dark, textured, tall, on a raised step → living Posidonia on living matte, the peat-like terrace of interwoven rhizomes it builds at one to six centimetres a year, metres thick and centuries old.

Pale, sparse, low, on flat sand → Cymodocea.

Smooth flat grey terrace, sharp cut edge, no shoots → dead matte. Shoots can be replanted; matte cannot be rebuilt on a human timescale, and once bare it erodes away.

Brilliant white patch, or a long straight white scar inside dark meadow → anchor damage, which you can count and photograph as you paddle. Anchoring on Posidonia is prohibited under Italian law.

Nobody tells visitors they can survey the condition of a protected habitat from a kayak with a snorkel mask. You can, and it takes no skill beyond these four lines.

Fish — the Mediterranean ones

  • ☐Ornate wrasse Thalassoma pavo — Green-turquoise crossed by narrow vertical orange-red bars; males add a blue head cut off from the body by a broad orange saddle, and a crescent-moon tail. Nervous, restless, hovering just above warm rock in the top three metres and never still. A thermophilic species whose spread north is one of the cleanest signals of Mediterranean warming.
  • ☐Rainbow wrasse Coris julis — Cigar-shaped, pointed snout, and two utterly different looks that are the same species: the female phase brown-red above and white below; the secondary male green-blue with a bright orange zigzag stripe and a black spot at the pectoral base. Protogynous — most individuals begin female and later transform — so this is the commonest identification trap on the route. Patrols ceaselessly, follows a snorkeller to snatch what you disturb, and buries itself in sand to sleep.
  • ☐Damselfish Chromis chromis — Oval, deeply forked tail; adults sooty brown-black, juveniles brilliant iridescent electric blue, and this is peak blue-juvenile season. Hangs in midwater shoals a metre or two above rock and "rains" back down the instant a shadow crosses.
  • ☐Salema Sarpa salpa — Silvery with about ten or eleven golden-yellow horizontal lines and a blunt sheep-like head. The only significant herbivorous fish here: it grazes Posidonia in tight, slow, synchronised shoals, all heads into the current, often in knee-deep water. On the famous hallucinogenic story: real, published, and vanishingly rare — a handful of case reports attributed to indole alkaloids from ingested algae concentrated in the viscera. Not a Salento phenomenon, and associated with eating the guts.
  • ☐Two-banded seabream Diplodus vulgaris — The easy one: exactly two conspicuous black bands, one behind the gill cover and one round the tail base, on a brassy body. Juveniles swarm the shallowest wave-washed rock. Its relatives: D. sargus (eight or nine narrow bars of alternating strength), D. annularis (black ring on the tail base and yellow pelvic fins), and saddled seabream Oblada melanura — plain silver, no bars, one black spot ringed in white at the tail base.
  • ☐Dusky grouper ★ Epinephelus marginatus — Heavy-bodied, huge mouth, brown with pale blotches it can switch on and off, and diagnostically a rounded tail with a distinct pale margin. Sits motionless in a cave mouth facing out, then either melts into the dark or — in a properly protected area — swims out to inspect you, and the tameness is itself the sign you are in a real reserve. Protogynous: the biggest fish are males. Italy's spearfishing prohibition plus reserve enforcement has produced marked recovery, with Torre Guaceto one of the cited successes. On the open coast, expect a distant glimpse.
  • ☐Brown meagre ★ Sciaena umbra — High-backed, dark bronze-brown with a metallic coppery sheen, and the best mark: brilliant white leading edges to the pelvic and anal fins. Hovers nose-down in loose groups in the shade of a cave entrance, barely moving. It drums, vibrating sonic muscles against the swim bladder; the spawning chorus is over by now but individual knocks still carry — put an ear to the deck near a cave mouth at dusk.
  • ☐Cardinalfish ★ Apogon imberbis — The signature fish of this coast. Small, bright orange-red, very large black eye, two separate dorsals, hanging oblique or upside-down in the dark of a cave roof in small groups; the eyes throw a torch beam back like coals. The male is a mouthbrooder. Look in every cave mouth from Otranto to Leuca.
  • ☐Mediterranean moray ★ Muraena helena — Thick snake-like body, dark brown with yellowish marbling, no pectoral fins, a single round gill hole. Head projecting from a hole with the mouth gaping rhythmically — which is breathing, not aggression. Bites happen when a hand goes into a hole, and only then.
  • ☐Painted comber Serranus scriba — Pale with five to seven dark vertical bars, blue-and-red "scribbled" markings on the cheek, and a violet-blue wash on the belly. Props itself on its pectoral fins on the bottom and moves in short hops. A simultaneous hermaphrodite — functionally male and female at once.
  • ☐Black scorpionfish ⚠ Scorpaena porcus — Dark mottled, with a long ragged skin tentacle above each eye. Absolute immobility, ambush, and total confidence in camouflage — it lets you come to a hand's width. Venomous dorsal spines: the risk is handling or putting a hand down, never the fish approaching you.
  • ☐Striped red mullet Mullus surmuletus — Pink-red with a dark stripe from eye to tail plus two or three yellow stripes, steep blunt forehead, two chin barbels. Probes the sand with the barbels and is usually shadowed by an opportunist wrasse stealing what it flushes — a whole food web in one glance.
  • ☐Common octopus ★ Octopus vulgaris — Find the midden first: a neat scatter of broken bivalve shells and crab carapaces at a hole mouth. Then eyes on raised turrets with a horizontal slit pupil, and skin that changes texture as well as colour.
  • ☐Common cuttlefish Sepia officinalis — Fin skirt running the full length of both sides, a W-shaped pupil, and a "zebra" display of dark transverse bands. Adults mostly die after spring spawning, so expect small juveniles — plus the reliable evidence, cuttlebones on every strandline.

Built onto the rock, and the three things not to touch

  • ☐Trottoir ★ ⚑ Lithophyllum byssoides — One of the best sights on the route and almost never pointed out. An encrusting coralline red alga that builds a hard rim or cornice precisely at mean sea level, projecting twenty to fifty centimetres and sometimes overhanging into a visor: a sharply defined pink-to-grey-white ledge exactly at the waterline, pitted, honeycombed, extremely abrasive. It grows about a millimetre a year, so a living trottoir is decades to centuries old — and a fossil trottoir stranded above present sea level is a palaeo-sea-level archive you can photograph. It will also shred hands and gel-coat.
  • ☐Cystoseira / Gongolaria ⚑ — Bushy brown "underwater trees" twenty to sixty centimetres tall forming a canopy on clean exposed rock at half a metre to three metres, swaying in the surge. Ecologically the Mediterranean's kelp-forest analogue, and highly sensitive to pollution, sedimentation and urchin grazing — so finding a healthy canopy is a strong statement about that stretch of coast. Losing it produces an urchin barren. Much of the genus has been split into Gongolaria and Ericaria, so older and newer books disagree.
  • ☐Purple and black sea urchins ⚠ Paracentrotus lividus / Arbacia lixula — One entry, because the pair separate instantly. Purple: dark purple to olive with long stout spines, wedged in a self-excavated pit and holding shell fragments and weed over itself like a hat — that debris habit is the field mark. Black: jet black, shorter finer spines, never carries debris, sitting on bare exposed vertical rock in the surge, and its overgrazing helps create the barrens. Both are wedged in every crevice at exactly the depth you put a foot down. Do not collect, and do not order them: a 2023 regional law closed the harvest and in 2026 Puglia extended the closure to 30 June 2029; Salento monitoring in 2025 found numbers and diameters recovering. Urchins on a menu that week are illegal.
  • ☐Bearded fireworm ⚠ Hermodice carunculata — Ten to thirty centimetres, flattened and segmented, orange-red to olive, with a conspicuous white bristle bundle on each side of every segment. It crawls slowly and openly across rock in broad daylight, which is exactly what catches people out, because it looks handleable. The calcareous bristles are hollow, detach on contact and drive into skin, burning for hours to days. Never touch it, and never brush or rub — that drives them deeper; lift them out with adhesive tape. Increasing rapidly with warming, and directly relevant because you will be hauling boats over rock all week.
  • ☐Weeverfish ⚠ Trachinus / Echiichthys spp. — Buried in Ionian sand with only the eyes and the tip of the black venomous first dorsal exposed. Standing on one is genuinely agonising; treatment is hot-water immersion at forty to forty-five degrees. The reason for stiff-soled shoes on the sandy side as well as the rocky one.

The absence in the meadow

  • ☐Fan mussel ★ ⚑ Pinna nobilis — You will see the ghost, not the animal. The Mediterranean's largest bivalve, to well over ninety centimetres, endemic, standing vertically in sand and seagrass on golden byssus threads. From autumn 2016 a mass mortality driven mainly by the parasite Haplosporidium pinnae swept the whole basin in three or four years, with site mortality of eighty-five to a hundred per cent; the species is now functionally extinct across most of its range and was reassessed as Critically Endangered in 2019. What remains in Salento meadows is empty valves still standing upright and sediment-filled, thin and brittle and iridescent inside — the most eloquent object in the meadow. Not quite hopeless: survivors are being genetically studied in the northern Adriatic under LIFE PINNA, and a citizen-science scheme from the CNR laboratory at Taranto, SOS Pinna, has collected reports of a small number of possibly resistant survivors in the gulf. If you find a live one — tissue visible in the gape, valves closing as your shadow passes — do not touch it, photograph it, record position and depth, and report it.
  • ☐Rough pen shell Pinna rudis — The useful half of that entry. Smaller, to about half a metre, with strong radial ribs bearing rows of large open tubular scales, and it lives on rock rather than seagrass on sand. It was far less affected by the die-off. So: ribbed and spiny on rock, possibly alive; smooth fan in seagrass, almost certainly a dead shell.
And the cave animals you cannot see, which are why the Zinzulusa is famous

Typhlocaris salentina — a blind, unpigmented shrimp in the anchialine pool at the back of the cave. Its only close relatives live in groundwater in Israel and in Cyrenaica. Blind cave shrimps cannot cross open sea, so the distribution is a Tethyan relict: the animals did not travel, the coastline did.

Spelaeomysis bottazzii — a blind stygobitic mysid crustacean endemic to Apulian groundwaters, in the same cave and in wells across the Salento. And Higginsia ciccaresei, a cave sponge described from this pool in the late 1990s: a new animal, from a ticketed Italian show cave, within living memory.

You will not see any of them on a walking tour. They are here because they are the best argument on this trip that biology is a record of geology.

Drifters and Stingers

Peak gelatinous season, and only one of them matters

Late September in the southern Adriatic is a high-biomass period for gelatinous plankton, but composition matters far more than abundance — and the happy arithmetic is that the two you will see most of are the two that barely sting, while the one that matters arrives on the wind rather than on the calendar.

  • ☐Mauve stinger ⚠ Pelagia noctiluca — The real hazard, and it is small. Bell only three to ten centimetres, mushroom-shaped, pink to mauve or lilac, with a warty, granular bell surface, eight long thin marginal tentacles and four frilly oral arms. Critically, it has stinging cells on the bell as well as the tentacles, so there is no safe part and glancing contact stings. Strongly bioluminescent. It is holoplanktonic — no bottom-living polyp stage, the whole life cycle offshore — which is precisely why swarms appear overnight with no local warning, delivered inshore by wind and current. The sting is immediate intense burning with linear whip-like weals, pain for hours, itching and hyperpigmentation for weeks. If you see three or four in a paddle, expect many more and think twice about swimming.
  • ☐Barrel jellyfish Rhizostoma pulmo — Big and harmless, worth knowing in advance because the size is alarming. Bell thirty to sixty centimetres and more, firm and whitish-blue, with a conspicuous violet-blue frilled band round the margin and no long tentacles at all — instead a dense cauliflower-like mass of eight fused oral arms. Extremely abundant in the Adriatic in autumn, sometimes in hundreds. You will paddle through them.
  • ☐Fried-egg jellyfish Cotylorhiza tuberculata — Unmistakable and at its annual peak on your dates: a smooth domed brownish-yellow central "yolk" in a flatter translucent rim, with short club-shaped appendages beneath tipped with white-violet buttons. Almost always with small fish sheltering underneath, which is the clinching behaviour. Sting negligible; routinely handled by local swimmers.
  • ☐By-the-wind sailor Velella velella — Not a jellyfish but a colonial hydrozoan: a flat oval deep-blue float with a transparent diagonal triangular sail set across it, which is what makes it sail downwind and what strands it in millions. Mass strandings here are mainly late winter and spring, so on your dates expect the dried "cellophane" sails crackling on the strandline rather than live animals.
  • ☐Portuguese man o' war ⚠ Physalia physalis — Extremely unlikely here, and included only so you can rule it out: an Atlantic species whose Mediterranean records cluster in the western basin, with a handful in the Adriatic. Learn the one line that separates it from Velella: a "Velella" ten to thirty centimetres long, with a big pink-and-blue crest along the top and threads trailing metres beneath, is not a Velella. Get out and warn people — including on the strandline, where a stranded one stings for days.
  • ☐Sea walnut Mnemiopsis leidyi — "The one you can hold." A transparent lobate ctenophore, five to ten centimetres, with two large trailing lobes and eight rows of comb plates that flicker with rainbow iridescence in sunlight. It has no stinging cells whatsoever and is completely harmless — an excellent teaching animal. Native to the western Atlantic, it wrecked the Black Sea anchovy fishery in the 1980s and has bloomed massively in the Adriatic since around 2016, eating fish eggs and larvae. A soupy layer you can scoop with a hand is entirely plausible now.
First aid: three things to stop doing

Vinegar is not a general treatment. Acetic acid is specific to Indo-Pacific box jellyfish. On Mediterranean species — Pelagia above all — it has been shown to trigger discharge of unfired stinging capsules in some species, making the sting worse.

Urine does nothing. Not sterile, wrong and variable osmolarity, and being hypotonic it can fire unfired capsules. Fresh water is actively harmful for the same reason; so is alcohol; so is rubbing with a towel or sand, because mechanical pressure discharges the rest.

Do this instead: out of the water; rinse copiously with seawater; lift adherent fragments off without pressure using the edge of a card or tweezers; then analgesia — hot immersion at about forty-two to forty-five degrees has the best trial evidence, a cold pack in a dry bag is the standard beach measure, and this is the single most disputed point in the field; then a topical steroid and an antihistamine, and expect delayed flares at the site for one to three weeks. Seek help for a large-area sting, anything on the face, or any systemic symptom.

And the ironic reversal: for sea urchin spines, vinegar genuinely does help, because acetic acid dissolves calcareous fragments. Soak in hot water for half an hour to an hour, lift out what protrudes, and do not dig or cut. The purple pigment tattoo left behind is harmless. So the kayak kit is: fine tweezers, a magnifier, adhesive tape, a plastic card, a small bottle of vinegar, hydrocortisone, an antihistamine, and thin gloves for hauling over rock.

Turtles, Whales and a Lost Seal

What is realistic from a kayak

The honest framing first. The inshore water you will be paddling is five to forty metres deep, and almost every Mediterranean cetacean except one is a deep-water animal needing the shelf edge or the Otranto Channel. A kayak's silence and low profile help at close range, but you cannot cover water, and marine-mammal spotting is a function of area searched. The realistic cetacean list from a kayak is one species long. The turtle list is much better than you would expect.

  • ☐Loggerhead turtle ★ Caretta caretta — Disproportionately large, broad, blocky head; five pairs of costal scutes and two pairs of prefrontal scales between the eyes; reddish-brown, often barnacled. From a kayak what you get is a fist-sized brown head appearing without warning at fifty to two hundred metres, an audible exhalation, a low brown shell-back, and a slow submergence — often only once. They also rest motionless at the surface in calm water, and a resting turtle will let a silent kayak approach far closer than a motorboat.
  • ☐A loggerhead nest ★ — Genuinely plannable, and worth asking about. The Ionian arc is the nesting coast. Italian nesting has climbed from 443 nests in 2023 to 601 in 2024 and over 700 in 2025 — a record — with about a hundred in Puglia, mostly in Lecce province, hatching concentrated in the Salento and at Porto Cesareo, coordinated through Legambiente's LIFE Turtlenest. Nesting runs May to August, incubation forty-five to seventy days, so hatching runs late July into October and you are in the tail of it. What you might find: a fenced and signed nest with a volunteer night watch; a hatchling emergence, at night or just before dawn; or a nest excavation a few days after, when volunteers open the chamber to count shells and rescue stragglers, usually delighted to explain. Rules: never dig; no white light on a nesting beach; never point a torch at a hatchling, since light disorientation is the main cause of hatchling death; report a fresh crawl rather than following it. Strandings: the coastguard, 1530. Green turtle, for completeness: nests in the eastern basin, a genuine rarity here — log any turtle as loggerhead unless you get a clear scute count.
  • ☐Bottlenose dolphin ★ Tursiops truncatus — Your one genuine chance. Robust, with a short thick beak creased at its base, uniform mid-grey with a subtle paler flank blaze, and a tall falcate dorsal. Coastal, in groups of three to fifteen, resident along both Puglian coasts and frequently associating with trawlers. Slow rolling group surfacings, occasional loud tail slaps, and deliberate bow-riding near boats. The striped and Risso's dolphins are offshore and deep-water animals: plausible in the Otranto Channel from a boat, not inshore from a kayak.
  • ☐Sperm whale ★ Physeter macrocephalus — Boat only, but genuinely there. The Gulf of Taranto holds a regularly observed presence including social units with calves, and is one of the better-documented sperm whale areas in the Ionian, with a long-running photo-identification and acoustic effort based out of Taranto. Diagnostic: a single bushy blow angled forward and to the left, a blocky wrinkled back with a low triangular hump instead of a dorsal fin, and the fluke lifted clear on the terminal dive. Half-day trips run out of Taranto — worth checking whether any coincide with your dates.
  • ☐Mediterranean monk seal ★ ⚑ Monachus monachus — One of the world's rarest mammals, a global population of the order of six to eight hundred animals cored in Greece and Turkey. Italian breeding was extinguished by the mid-twentieth century, the last records in the 1970s. But the coast you are paddling documents its own lost fauna in its place-names: caves called Grotta del Bove Marino — "cave of the sea ox," the vernacular for the monk seal — recur along the Otranto–Leuca shore, and you can find them on the chart before you launch. Since roughly the 2010s vagrants have been reappearing in Italian waters including the Adriatic, dispersing from a recovering Greek population, and individuals have been photographed in southern Italian sea caves. The caves here remain structurally suitable: high-tide entrances, internal beaches, darkness. Honest odds in one week: essentially zero — but not structurally impossible, and this is exactly where it would happen. If it does: do not approach, do not enter the cave, do not post the location, photograph from distance, and report it.

Birds Worth the Binoculars

The ones you cannot already name

You know the finches and the crows, so this chapter is short and selective: the Mediterranean specialities, the endemic races, the autumn migrants that are genuinely moving through Salento in the last week of September, and one honest miss. Two structural facts shape it. Shearwaters are horizon birds from a kayak — the highest-yield seawatching on the route is standing at Punta Palascìa at dawn with binoculars, especially after a night of northerly wind. And the Strait of Otranto is only partly the bottleneck it is sold as: it concentrates migrants, but far more in spring, when birds arriving from Greece and Albania make landfall, than in autumn, when Salento is a departure point. Do not expect a Messina-style spectacle.

  • ☐European shag ★ ⚑ Gulosus aristotelis desmarestii — The Mediterranean race, and genuinely gettable from a kayak on the Otranto–Leuca cliffs. Smaller and slimmer than a cormorant, all dark with an oily bottle-green gloss, a steep forehead and a thin hooked bill; desmarestii adds a yellow gape and pale chin and a longer thinner neck. The one line worth memorising: a "cormorant" with a white belly on a Salento rock is a young desmarestii shag. It swims very low and jumps clear of the water in a forward arc before diving, where a great cormorant simply slides under. Note also that great cormorant is largely a winter visitor here whose first flocks arrive from late September into October — precisely your week — so the cormorants at Alimini and Le Cesine are freshly arrived.
  • ☐Scopoli's shearwater ★ ⚑ Calonectris diomedea — Large and long-winged, pale sandy-grey-brown above and white below, with a yellow bill with a dark tip and diagnostically no clean cap: the brown of the head washes diffusely into the white throat with no border. Flight relaxed and unhurried, long lazy glides on bowed, slightly kinked wings. It finishes breeding through September and then leaves the Mediterranean for the Atlantic, so a late-September bird is on the departing tail.
  • ☐Yelkouan shearwater ★ ⚑ Puffinus yelkouan — Much smaller, brown and white, with a dark cap extending below the eye, a dark bill, and the key mark: a dirty, dusky vent and undertail. Flight fast, stiff and whirring with short low glides, hugging the water, usually in flocks. An Adriatic and Aegean speciality that disperses within the basin rather than leaving it, so it becomes relatively more likely as autumn advances — your dates sit on the crossover, with Scopoli's the better bet on Day 1 and Yelkouan by Day 7. The Italian Adriatic colonies of both are on the Tremiti islands, not the Salento.
  • ☐Audouin's gull ★ ⚑ Ichthyaetus audouinii — The gull prize, and unlikely, but check every candidate. Pale grey above with a white head, a coral-red bill with a black band near the tip and a yellow tip, dark olive-grey to blackish legs, a dark eye, and a gently sloping forehead giving a curiously gentle expression. The behaviour is diagnostic: it hunts live fish by dipping and flying low like a big tern, and rarely scavenges — an elegant gull that will not be at the bar. A Mediterranean endemic, scarce in Puglia.
  • ☐Mediterranean gull ⚑ Ichthyaetus melanocephalus — Realistic, and the comparison with black-headed gull is the entry: autumn adults have pure white primaries — no black in the wingtip at all, a heavier slightly drooping red-and-black bill, longer legs, and a dark bandit-mask smudge behind the eye. Nests colonially in the Adriatic including the northern Puglian salt pans.
  • ☐Pallid swift ★ Apus pallidus — The most useful non-obvious call of the week. Browner and paler than common swift, with a larger diffuse pale throat patch, blunter wing-tips, and a scaly pale-fringed look to the underparts. But the decisive argument is phenology: pallid swift is double-brooded and late, still feeding young into September and lingering into October, whereas common swift has largely left Italy by mid-August. So a swift screaming round the old town of Otranto, Gallipoli or Lecce now is almost certainly this — and Salento is a stronghold, with colonies in the walls and cornices.
  • ☐Blue rock thrush ★ Monticola solitarius — Very achievable. The male is uniform slate-blue, appearing black at distance, slim with a long straight bill. The behaviour is diagnostic: sits bolt upright on the highest point of a cliff, a tower or a ruin, flicks its wings, dips its tail, then drops vertically out of sight. Silent at this season. The Otranto–Leuca cliffs, the coastal towers, and the walls at Egnazia.
  • ☐Crag martin Ptyonoprogne rupestris — Stocky, plain brown, pale-throated, with dark undertail coverts contrasting against the pale breast and the clincher, a row of small white spots across the spread tail, visible whenever it fans. Flies slowly and deliberately, hugging the rock face and banking hard against it. A short-distance migrant, so present now.
  • ☐Osprey ★ Pandion haliaetus — Prime time: mid-September to October, and a genuinely realistic sighting from a kayak or over Alimini. A white head with a broad dark eye-stripe, long arched gull-like wings with black carpal patches, white underbody. Hovers heavily, plunges feet-first, and shakes itself in mid-air after rising from a dive.
  • ☐Pallid harrier ★ Circus macrourus — A genuine rarity that has become a regularly recorded autumn migrant in Italy, and mid-September to October is exactly the window — one where your dates help. The juvenile is the identification: like Montagu's, but with a pale collar or "boa" set off by dark neck-sides and a dark cheek patch, and a more contrasting face. Marsh harrier, by contrast, is simply likely: long-winged in a shallow V, quartering low over the reeds at Le Cesine and Alimini, females and juveniles dark chocolate with a creamy crown, throat and forewing patch.
  • ☐Red-footed falcon ★ Falco vespertinus — Plausible on your dates; the Adriatic flyway carries flocks through September. A small long-winged falcon hawking insects at dusk over stubble, often in loose groups. The male is slate-grey with rusty-red thighs and vent and red feet and cere.
  • ☐Greater flamingo ★ Phoenicopterus roseus — Realistic, and Puglia has gone from scarce visitor to regular and locally breeding; the salt pans at Salina dei Monaci and Torre Colimena on the Ionian side are the easiest on the route. Give the behavioural marks rather than the colour: the sharply kinked, downturned bill used upside down with the head inverted below the surface; the "broken" angled neck at rest; the sudden reveal of crimson-and-black wings on take-off; and the greyest birds in the flock, with dull grey bills and dark legs, are this year's young. While you are at a wetland, look also for glossy ibis ★, now regular in Italy and expanding: at distance a black, long-legged, down-curved-billed wader; in good light, bronze and iridescent green-purple, flying in lines with fast flapping and glides rather than folding its neck like a heron.
  • ☐Lesser kestrel ★ Falco naumanni — The honest miss, and worth knowing why. Smaller and daintier than kestrel, the male with an unspotted brick-red mantle, a plain blue-grey head with no moustachial stripe, and a blue-grey panel in the greater coverts; colonial, noisy, insectivorous, hawking rather than hovering. Puglia holds the largest Italian population — thousands of pairs nesting under the roof tiles of the Alta Murgia towns, Gravina, Altamura, Santeramo — and it is one of the great wildlife spectacles of southern Italy. But it is a trans-Saharan migrant that gathers in pre-migratory roosts in late August and has largely departed by mid-September. You have missed it by a fortnight, and the Murgia is inland from your route anyway. File under: reasons to come back in June — along with the roller, for which the Murgia and Salento are a European stronghold with active nestbox schemes, and which is also gone by early September.
The wetlands, if a windy morning frees a day

Le Cesine — WWF reserve and Ramsar site behind the dunes near San Foca, minutes from Torre dell'Orso, and the highest-value wildlife stop on the route: reedbed, brackish lagoon and pinewood with hides and trails, and in late September the first wintering ducks, marsh harrier, waders on the mud, squacco and purple and night herons, glossy ibis, kingfisher, and enormous hirundine roosts in the reeds at dusk.

Laghi Alimini, north of Otranto — one lake brackish and sea-connected, one spring-fed, with superb protected dune juniper woodland and the best raptor-watching airspace on the route. Palude del Capitano on the Ionian side, for the spunnulate — karst collapse dolines filled with brackish groundwater — and for Porto Selvaggio's cold freshwater karst springs discharging straight into the sea, which you feel from the kayak as a shock of cold. And Torre Guaceto, the marine protected area on the transit north, which has Italy's best-documented reserve fish-recovery record and is the one place on the route where a grouper might swim out to look at you.

Ask the evening before, not at breakfast: it needs driving and a decision.

On Land

Reptiles, bats, and the viper question

The richest habitat on this route is a human artefact. A Salento or Valle d'Itria dry-stone wall — the craft is on the UNESCO intangible heritage list — is simultaneously a lizard and gecko basking bank, a snake highway and hibernaculum, a skink refuge, a marten and dormouse run, a little owl perch, and a cyclamen and fern microhabitat on its shaded north face. Add the cisterns, where the newts live, and the point becomes general: the most biodiverse structures here are the ones people built, which is why their abandonment and their bulldozing are both conservation problems. The rule that follows: turn stones, and put them back exactly as they were.

  • ☐Italian wall lizard ⚑ Podarcis siculus — An Italian and Adriatic endemic, and the default lizard of every wall, dune and ruin here. Bright green over at least the front half of the back, patterned with dark reticulation, fairly robust with a deep head. The negative marks matter: it lacks the strong dark lateral band of the common wall lizard, and lacks blue outer belly-scale spots. Bold, out in the open on hot stone, and remarkably tolerant — at Egnazia they will run over your feet. Essentially every wall lizard you see will be this; P. muralis is not to be expected in lowland Salento.
  • ☐Ocellated skink ★ Chalcides ocellatus — A genuine Salento speciality and a good find. Cylindrical, glossy, sausage-shaped with short weak legs, the surface almost polished sandy-bronze with many small black-edged white ocelli in rows. The behaviour is the mark: it does not run like a lizard, it wriggles snake-like into leaf litter, loose sand or a wall crevice — and is far more often heard as a rustle at the base of a dry-stone wall than seen.
  • ☐Moorish and Turkish geckos Tarentola mauritanica / Hemidactylus turcicus — One entry, one rule. Moorish: large, to about fifteen centimetres, grey-brown, heavily armoured with big conical keeled tubercles in longitudinal rows, broad flat triangular head, sitting head-down and fully exposed on a vertical wall, often out in daylight — bulky and confident. Turkish: small and delicate, translucent pinkish-cream and often visibly see-through, fine soft granular tubercles, large dark eyes, strictly nocturnal, keeping to crevices and the wall around a light — and it calls with a squeaky chirp. The mouse squeaking in the masonry at night is the second one. Bulky, warty, bold, grey = Moorish; small, pale, translucent, squeaky, shy = Turkish.
  • ☐Western whip snake Hierophis viridiflavus — The snake you will actually meet. Slender, up to a metre and a half, large eyes and a round pupil, whip-thin tail. South-eastern Italy is dominated by the melanistic form: a uniformly glossy blue-black snake. Explosively fast — it crosses a masseria track as a black ribbon — and it will stand its ground, inflate, hiss and strike repeatedly. Harmless, though it can draw blood, and it makes a startling loud rattle through dry vegetation.
  • ☐Four-lined snake ★ Elaphe quatuorlineata — Italy's largest snake, and Puglia is a European stronghold. A metre and a half to two metres, notably heavy-bodied, thick and slow-moving, with a proportionately small head. Adults straw-brown to ochre with four dark longitudinal stripes — only two visible from above — and a dark stripe through the eye. Juveniles look like a different animal: strongly grey with dark saddles and blotches, which is exactly why they get killed as vipers. Famously docile, rarely attempts to bite, climbs into scrub and walls, and raids rodent burrows and nests. The classic masseria "chicken snake," listed on Annex II of the Habitats Directive.
The viper question — and a correction to an appealing myth

You will be told there are no vipers in the Salento. It is very nearly true, and it is not true. Since this is exactly the sort of claim a single record overturns, here is the actual position.

Italy has four viper species, and three of them — the adder, Orsini's viper and the nose-horned viper — occur nowhere near Puglia. So the question is entirely about the asp viper in its southern form, Vipera aspis hugyi. It is present in Puglia on the Gargano and the Murgia. And it is also recorded in the Salento — but as one of the peninsula's rarest reptiles: ecologically selective, tied to genuinely intact habitat, and with a punctiform distribution of a handful of known localities rather than a population.

The awkward part, for a book that would prefer to reassure you, is where those localities are. On the Adriatic side they are given as San Cataldo, Otranto and Santa Cesarea; on the Ionian side, the Arneo and Ugento. Which is to say: your Day 3 lunch stop, the town you walk round that evening, and quite possibly your Day 4 beach. The best-known population, at Frassanito just north of Otranto, appears to be the only one holding decent numbers and has been the subject of a dedicated field study. Local papers treat a sighting as news precisely because it is so uncommon.

So the honest framing. The overwhelming majority of snakes you meet will be the glossy blue-black whip snake or the heavy, docile four-lined snake, and neither is venomous; a snake crossing a track is still almost certainly good news and worth stopping to look at properly. But almost is doing real work in that sentence, and the conclusion is the one that was always sensible: look before you put a hand into a dry-stone wall, and look before you reach under a rock or into an overhang you cannot see behind. That rule costs nothing and it covers vipers, centipedes and fireworms at once.

On the venom, since the point of knowing is to be calm rather than frightened: an aspis bite is a medical event and not usually a grave one, dangerous chiefly to children, the frail, or anyone who delays treatment. The animal is small, retiring, and would much rather you went away.

  • ☐Hermann's tortoise ★ ⚑ Testudo hermanni — The marks that separate it from the spur-thighed tortoise are at the back end: a divided, two-part supracaudal scute, a horny spur at the very tip of the tail, and no enlarged tubercle on the thigh. Present in macchia, garrigue and abandoned olive groves, though heavily reduced by fire, deep ploughing and collection. Non-obvious and useful: late September and October are among the better times to find one. Tortoises aestivate through the worst summer heat and become active again when the first autumn rain breaks it — so a warm morning after rain on your dates is a genuine chance. Walk slowly and listen for a heavy rustle in dry leaf litter.
  • ☐Italian newt ★ ⚑ Lissotriton italicus — Endemic to central-southern Italy and a lovely Salento speciality. Very small, seven or eight centimetres, olive-brown with a yellow-orange unspotted or lightly spotted belly and throat and a dark stripe through the eye. Look in masseria cisterns, stone water troughs and shaded tanks with a torch while everyone else is at dinner. Its amphibian neighbours worth naming: the green toad Bufotes balearicus, an Italian-and-islands endemic, pale grey-cream with bold irregular olive-green marbling and a long musical trill unlike any other Italian amphibian, a dune and garden animal; and the Italian tree frog Hyla intermedia, endemic, bright leaf-green with a dark flank stripe, mostly silent now but calling after rain.
  • ☐Crested porcupine ★ Hystrix cristata — Unmistakable if seen, but the realistic encounter is indirect: shed quills on tracks — pick one up, hollow, light, astonishingly stiff — conical feeding pits dug for roots and bulbs, and cleanly gnawed prickly-pear pads. Strictly nocturnal; when alarmed it rattles its hollow rear quills and reverses into the threat. Native or introduced? Genuinely contested, and the open question is more interesting than a false answer: the majority view is an ancient human-mediated introduction from North Africa, plausibly Roman-era, supported by low genetic differentiation from North African populations, while Italian Pleistocene Hystrix fossils are attributed to different, extinct species. Fully protected regardless, and expanding strongly in Puglia.
  • ☐Garden dormouse ★ Eliomys quercinus — A bold black bandit mask through and behind the eye, very large mobile ears, clean white below, and the clincher, a long tail ending in a flattened black-and-white brush. Lives in walls, outbuildings and orchards, and is aggressive and vocal: a sudden chattering shriek from a fig tree at night is very likely this. The other night noise is the stone marten, identified by a creamy-white throat bib, usually forked and running onto the forelegs, and a pinkish nose — and it is what is thumping about above your bedroom at two in the morning.
  • ☐European free-tailed bat ★ Tadarida teniotis — The best bat entry available, because you can identify it without a detector. Huge — thirty-five to forty centimetres of wingspan — cigar-shaped, with enormous forward-projecting ears and a long tail protruding well beyond the membrane, in fast high straight flight over cliffs and towns. And its call is low enough to be audible to the unaided ear: a hard metallic tsick… tsick from high overhead at dusk. Stand outside after dinner and listen up.
  • ☐Cave bats, and why the month matters Miniopterus schreibersii and others — The sea caves hold roosts: Schreiber's bent-winged bat above all, strictly cave-dwelling year-round and forming dense ceiling clusters, with horseshoe bats and the Puglian speciality Myotis capaccinii, which trawls the water surface with disproportionately large feet. Late September is the worst possible time to disturb them. Mediterranean bats are in pre-hibernation hyperphagia and autumn swarming, laying down the fat that determines whether they survive the winter; a flushed cluster burns energy it cannot replace. So: no torches on ceilings, no shouting or whistling to test the echo — a standard sea-cave habit and a genuinely damaging one — no flash, stay near the entrance, and turn back if you smell ammonia or see a guano floor.

The False Autumn

Why almost everything in flower is a leafless bulb

This is the most interesting botanical fact of your dates, and it reverses northern expectations completely. Late September in Salento is not autumn. It is the last week of a nine-month drought, and almost the entire flora is dormant, brown and shut down. The consequence is that nearly everything in flower is a leafless geophyte — flowering on a stored bulb, triggered by heat or day-length or the first rain, and putting up its leaves only afterwards. Two of the most conspicuous shrubs on the cliffs stand bare because it is dry, not cold, and are about to come into leaf. And the whole system is poised on the first serious rain: if it breaks while you are there, the landscape changes visibly within forty-eight hours — a green flush, fungi, amphibians calling, tortoises walking, the first orchid rosettes.

  • ☐Sea squill ★ Drimia maritima — The plant of your dates, conspicuous at two hundred metres across a burnt hillside. A massive bulb, often half-exposed above ground, throwing up a single completely leafless spike a metre to a metre and a half tall, packed with dozens to hundreds of small white stars each with a green midrib, opening from the bottom upward over several weeks. The broad glaucous basal rosette appears afterwards, through autumn and winter. Three extras: it is highly toxic — cardiac glycosides — and was the classic Mediterranean rat poison; it is grazing-resistant, so abundance indicates heavy grazing and disturbance; and it was traditionally planted to mark field boundaries and graves, so a straight line of squill spikes across a Salento field may literally be a property line.
  • ☐Autumn squill Prospero autumnale — Small, five to twenty centimetres, a slender spike of a few pale lilac-violet stars with no bracts, flowering before the thread-like leaves appear, in short grazed turf and the thin soil of the limestone shelf. Genuinely in flower now — but you have to be looking down, and it is exactly the sort of thing people walk over all week. Look also for autumn crocus, Colchicum: leafless pink-lilac goblets emerging straight out of bare ground, separated from a true crocus by having six stamens and three separate styles where a crocus has three stamens and one style. The region's true autumn crocuses, including a southern Puglian endemic, flower mainly in October and November, so the very earliest could just overlap your last days.
  • ☐Ivy-leaved cyclamen ★ Cyclamen hederifolium — Pink reflexed flowers with a dark magenta mouth, on twisted stalks, appearing before the leaves; the ivy-shaped silver-marbled leaves follow. Late September is the very start of its season — best in shade under holm oak and along old walls in the wooded Valle d'Itria. Include the behaviour, because it is remarkable: after flowering the stalk coils into a tight spring, drawing the capsule down to ground level, where ants carry the sticky seeds away.
  • ☐Autumn lady's-tresses ★ Spiranthes spiralis — The one orchid you could plausibly find. Ten to twenty centimetres, with a single spiral rank of small white-and-green flowers and a separate basal leaf rosette off to one side, in short grazed turf, flowering August to October. Worth saying plainly what the alternative is: Salento and the Murgia are of European importance for orchids, with a spectacular Ophrys flora including several narrow Apulian endemics — and the entire season runs February to May. In late September you will see nothing else in flower, at best the emerging basal rosettes from October. If orchids matter, the month is April.
  • ☐Vallonea oak ★ ⚑ Quercus ithaburensis subsp. macrolepis — You are having dinner at Tricase on Day 2, and the monumental tree is a few minutes outside the town: the Quercia dei Cento Cavalieri, the Oak of the Hundred Knights, with a crown spread quoted around seven hundred square metres and an age quoted at seven to nine hundred years — both traditional rather than measured. The diagnostic feature is the acorn cup: enormous, woody, covered in long recurved reflexed scales like a shaggy crown, unlike any other European oak. That cup is the vallonea of commerce, exported in bulk for tanning leather. Acorns take two years to ripen, in September and October, so your dates are exactly right to pick one up off the ground. And the real story is the phytogeography: an eastern-Mediterranean species whose only native Italian stands are in the Salento — a relict of a Greek and Anatolian flora stranded on the heel of Italy.
  • ☐Macedonian oak — the fragno ★ ⚑ Quercus trojana — The companion fact for the Valle d'Itria. This deciduous oak is the signature tree of the Murge, and its natural western limit in the world lies here — the woods around Ceglie Messapica and Martina Franca are the westernmost stands anywhere. Its wood is what the Martina Franca capocollo is smoked over.
  • ☐Thorny burnet ★ ⚑ Sarcopoterium spinosum — The third relict, and the one that completes the pattern. A dense, perfectly hemispherical, intricately spiny dwarf shrub thirty to sixty centimetres high, with tiny pinnate leaves and zig-zag spine-tipped twigs. Like the tree spurge it is summer-deciduous, leafing out with the autumn rains. An eastern-Mediterranean species restricted in Italy to a handful of Puglian localities — so its Salento garrigue is effectively a stranded fragment of Greek and Levantine landscape, matching the Vallonea oak exactly. It indicates fire, grazing and thin degraded karst soil.
  • ☐Tree spurge ★ Euphorbia dendroides — An inverted seasonality worth pointing out explicitly. A dome of bare grey-white forking stems through late summer, because it is deciduous in response to drought, not cold, and it re-greens with the autumn rains. The dead-looking bushes on the cliffs are the ones about to come into leaf.
  • ☐Sticky fleabane Dittrichia viscosa — The single most conspicuous flower of your dates, and almost always omitted from guidebooks. A sticky, intensely aromatic yellow-daisy subshrub covering every roadside, field margin and disturbed slope from September to November, and a major late-season nectar source for the last butterflies and hoverflies. If one plant defines late-September Salento visually, this is it.
  • ☐Rock samphire Crithmum maritimum — It grows exactly where you land a kayak: succulent, fleshy, blue-green divided leaves in salt-spray crevices, with greenish-yellow umbels, and strongly, pungently resinous when crushed. On your dates you get flowers and fruit on the same plant. It marks the aerohaline zone, immediately above the Lithophyllum trottoir — so you can photograph the entire vertical zonation of the shore in one frame from the boat. Beside it on the rock shelf, sea lavenders ⚑ (Limonium), papery pink-violet sprays flowering into autumn, and several narrow Salento and Apulian endemics sit in that genus — which makes an unremarkable-looking plant one of the region's real botanical distinctions.
  • ☐Mastic Pistacia lentiscus — The structural shrub of the macchia, diagnosed on the leaf: pinnate with an EVEN number of leaflets and no terminal leaflet, on a conspicuously winged, grooved rachis, strongly resinous when crushed. Fruit ripens red then black exactly now, and the black-fruit stage is what you will see, being stripped by blackcaps, robins and Sardinian warblers. The shrub-and-warbler mutualism is the entry: the migrant warblers arriving this week are here largely because the lentisc is fruiting.
  • ☐Carob ★ Ceratonia siliqua — Dark glossy pinnate leaves and magnificently gnarled trunks, with long flattened leathery brown pods to twenty centimetres ripening now — you can pick fallen pods up. Two things: the seeds are famously uniform in weight and are the origin of the carat; and it has an odd flowering habit, with small red petal-less flowers borne directly on old wood in autumn, with a strong and frankly unpleasant smell — so you may find the same tree flowering and fruiting simultaneously.
Small life, since it is what is visible in a drought

Two-tailed pasha Charaxes jasius ★ — the largest European butterfly, and its second brood flies August to October, so this is its month. Orange-banded on a dark ground with two tails on each hindwing, powerfully fast and aggressively territorial. The larva feeds on strawberry tree; the adults come to fallen figs and to dung, so look at rotting fruit rather than at flowers.

Oleander hawkmoth Daphnis nerii — the reason to look at every oleander hedge: the caterpillar is large, green, with a blue-and-white eyespot behind the head. And hummingbird hawkmoth hovering at the Dittrichia in numbers. Plus praying mantis at its largest now, and the extraordinary cone-headed Empusa pennata.

And the spider that probably caused tarantism. Salento's pizzica tarantata is usually presented as pure folklore, but there is a zoological argument underneath. The ritual was blamed on the tarantula wolf spider Lycosa tarantula — large, burrowing, and with a trivial bite. The far more plausible culprit is the Mediterranean black widow Latrodectus tredecimguttatus: black, marked with thirteen red spots, living in dry grassland and cereal stubble exactly where harvest labourers worked, and possessing a genuinely neurotoxic venom capable of producing the sweating, cramps and prolonged distress in the historical accounts. A famous piece of culture, a misidentification that lasted centuries, and two spiders you can go and look for in the stubble. Do not handle the second.

Five Things a Naturalist Would Notice

And that no guidebook mentions

One. The inverted calendar. This is not autumn but the end of a nine-month drought, and the countryside runs backwards from northern expectations: shrubs leafless because it is dry, everything in flower a leafless bulb, tortoises emerging from aestivation rather than entering hibernation. The whole system is poised on the first rain, and if it breaks in front of you the change is visible within two days.

Two. From a kayak you are flying over a habitat map, and reading the damage. The clarity makes the surface a window, and four signatures do all the work — living meadow, Cymodocea, dead matte, anchor scar. You can survey the condition of a protected habitat from a sit-on-top with a snorkel mask, and count the scars as you go. Read the water with your hands too: along the Otranto–Leuca coast and at Porto Selvaggio, cold freshwater karst springs discharge directly into the sea, and you will feel the layer and see the optical shimmer of the halocline. That is the aquifer that supplies the region's drinking water, arriving in the sea underneath you.

Three. Late September is the acoustic changing of the guard, and you can document it in three notes a night. The cicadas fall silent and the bush-crickets and tree crickets take over. The scops owl has stopped calling and gone to Africa, leaving the little owl and the barn owl in possession. The swifts overhead have changed species — common swift gone since August, pallid swift still screaming round the old towns into October. And the first robins tick in the masseria garden while the first black redstarts appear on the walls, both being winter arrivals in the far south. If you note nothing else each evening — which owl, which insect, which chat — you will have recorded the turn of the Mediterranean year more precisely than any calendar does.

Four. The strandline is the best notebook on the route. Two hundred metres in late September will typically give you, in one walk: Posidonia aegagropiles and banquette litter; cuttlebones; the corky black floating seeds of the sea daffodil; the dried crackling sails of Velella; the buoyant green "sea olives" of Posidonia after a flowering year; mermaid's purses, the egg cases of catsharks and skates, your direct evidence that there are still sharks and rays offshore; and the empty spires of the two murex snails from which Tyrian purple was extracted. That last is the best archaeology-meets-biology join on the trip: at Egnazia you can paddle over the submerged Roman port and then pick up, off the beach, the shell of the animal that made the empire's purple.

Five. Keep the warming tally. Salento is a front line of Mediterranean tropicalisation, and you can keep a list of species that would have been remarkable thirty years ago and are now ordinary: ornate wrasse in abundance; the fireworm; the invasive alga Caulerpa cylindracea and the Red Sea seagrass Halophila stipulacea; the blue crab, now a major and bitterly contested invasion in Italian lagoons; and the Lessepsian migrants moving west out of the Suez route, including the silver-cheeked toadfish — seriously toxic, must not be eaten, and its records actively wanted — the bluespotted cornetfish, and rabbitfish. Any of these should be photographed and reported to the Italian citizen-science schemes, which turns a paddling holiday into data. For several of these species, tourists with cameras are how the range front is actually mapped.
